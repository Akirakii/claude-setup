Cheap games that paid

Which indie games made money without years of work, what they had in common, and what that means for the next project. Compares recent co-op and single-player hits and ranks 14 genres by return against effort and risk.

2026-11-0127 games · 14 genres5 research passes

Sales are mostly third-party estimates (Gamalytic, Raijin, VG Insights, AppMagic, Sensor Tower) and can be off by 2× or more; developer-announced figures are marked “dev”. Every game here is a survivor, so read the base rates before the hits.

Summary

Five findings

  1. 1

    The usual outcome is near zero. About 20,000 games came out on Steam in 2025; about two thirds earned under $1,000 and about 3% reached 1,000 reviews.

  2. 2

    Co-op hits are the fastest to build (2–5 months) but almost never by a solo first-timer. They come from 2–10 person teams with earlier games, reused netcode or an audience. The one solo co-op hit, Lethal Company, came after 19 earlier games.

  3. 3

    Single-player quick hits are often solo (2 weeks to 13 months) and share one formula: a proven loop plus one twist a 5-second clip can show, cheap stylized art, a $3–10 price, and a demo or itch build before launch.

  4. 4

    Copies without a twist flop. Only one survivors-like reached 1,000 reviews in 2024. Bramble Royale made $8,000 in its first week: “no marketable hook”.

  5. 5

    Practice is the hidden ingredient. Almost every quick hit came after many earlier games. What repeats is a series of small games, each kept or dropped on demo response, not one big bet.

For a solo dev, single player has the better effort-to-risk ratio. Co-op’s speed comes from teams and reused tech. The solo dev of Garden Wars said multiplayer “makes everything take 5 times as long”, and every co-op hit needed launch-week fixes for griefing or hackers. Co-op stays an option only as a tiny jam-sized game built on a Steam P2P template (GodotSteam or Photon) from day one.

Co-op flops

  • “Dozens” of Lethal Company clones; only R.E.P.O. broke out.

Single-player flops

  • Survivors-likes reaching 1,000 reviews: 10 in 2022, 17 in 2023, 1 in 2024. Only big twists broke through in 2025.
  • Bramble Royale (deckbuilder): $8,049 in week one from 4,117 wishlists. “No marketable hook”, “people are fatigued on deckbuilders”.
  • JokerJack: reviewed as a “derivative, barely functional copycat”.
  • Archetype: Portal to Hell (solo, under a year): 24 copies. No hook; store page up only weeks before launch.

Six lessons

  1. One proven loop plus one twist. Peggle became Peglin and Nubby’s; Balatro became CloverPit; Vampire Survivors became Megabonk; Motherload became Digging a Hole; idle plus farm became Rusty’s. Only Vampire Survivors invented a genre.
  2. The 5-second clip test. If a short clip doesn’t explain the game and show a funny, tense or “broken” moment, streamers won’t pick it up. Games that look hard to learn die.
  3. Cheap stylized art and a $3–10 price. PS1 low-poly, pixel, UI-only or licensed assets. A low price excuses a short game.
  4. Prove demand before finishing. An itch build, a demo, Next Fest, daily short clips. Weak demo numbers are the cheapest signal to stop.
  5. Expect a spike, then a drop. Revenue comes up front. Plan a finished game, not a live service.
  6. Practice is the hidden ingredient. Almost every quick hit followed many earlier games. The repeatable strategy is several small games.
For the next project

Shortlist

Single player, 3–6 months of planned scope (which tends to become about a year), priced $5–10.

1 · Survivors-like with a big twist

Natural on a gamepad, fits the Switch, clips well. Could reuse the Automate swarm from Assemblia. Plain clones flop, so the twist must be large.

2 · Toy roguelike around a familiar object

A Balatro-like built on something everyone knows (CloverPit used a slot machine). Cheap art; the risk is design and balance, not content.

3 · Short anomaly or one-room horror

The cheapest (3–4 months), and a PS1 look suits Meshy output. Streamers love it, but the typical game earns little. Works as a first release before a bigger game.

Recommendation: treat this as a series of small bets. Start with the cheapest option you’d enjoy making, prove the clip and the demo within a few weeks, and keep going only if the demo pulls players.

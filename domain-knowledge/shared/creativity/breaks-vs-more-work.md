# Creativity: more work or a break? (researched 2026-11-19)

Asked by the user: "im confused if i need to do more work or less for creativity.. is this researched".
All rows are from abstracts and search summaries; no full paper was read.

## Short answer
Both, in order: work hard on the problem first, take a short easy break once stuck, then come back.
The break doesn't do the thinking; it mostly clears the stuck approach.

## Findings
| Claim | Supported? | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| A break helps on a problem you're stuck on | ✅ | Sio & Ormerod 2009, meta-analysis of 117 studies; the effect is small to moderate (d = 0.29 per a later review) [1][2] |
| More work on the problem before the break makes the break help more | ✅ | same meta-analysis: longer preparation gave a larger effect [1] |
| An easy activity in the break beats a demanding one | ✅ | same meta-analysis; for word problems it also beat plain rest [1][2] |
| Idea-generating tasks gain more from a break than insight puzzles | ✅ | same meta-analysis [2] |
| The break works because the stuck approach fades | ✅ | experiments by Smith and others ("forgetting fixation") [3] |
| The break works because the open problem gets matched to things you run into | ✅ | supported, but mostly from anecdotes and correlations ("opportunistic assimilation") [4] |
| The unconscious actively works the problem during the break | ❌ | the least supported of the three; Seabrook & Dienes 2003 say research has not supported it [4]; the related claim that distraction beats deliberation for decisions failed a large replication (Nieuwenstein 2015, N = 399) [5] |
| Mind wandering is why easy breaks help | ❌ | Baird 2012 claimed it; Murray 2024 (two studies, N = 443) and Smeekens & Kane 2016 did not find it [6] |
| Most important ideas come while working, not away from work | ✅ | Gable, Hopper & Schooler 2019: about 1 in 5 important ideas of physicists and writers came while thinking of something unrelated; self-reported [7] |
| Producing more ideas gives more good ideas | ✅ partly | Simonton's equal-odds rule; data mostly from eminent creators, and later studies are mixed [8] |

## What Claude draws from it (not researched)
- Playing and studying shipped games to sharpen the sense of fun fits "more preparation helps", but the studies measured work on the one problem, not general exposure. (Claude)

## The kinds of stuck (researched 2026-11-19, same limits: abstracts and summaries)
Asked by the user: "i can be stuck because im lacking working hard".
| Claim | Supported? | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| There is a kind of stuck that comes from a wrong starting picture of the problem, after a period of real work | ✅ | Knoblich, Ohlsson et al. 1999: the way out is dropping an assumption you didn't know you held, and repeated failure is what triggers that [9] |
| A break helps only this kind | ✅ | Smith & Blankenship 1991: every break benefit they found depended on first getting the person fixed on a wrong answer [10] |
| There is a kind of stuck that is just stopping too early | ✅ | Lucas & Nordgren 2015, 7 studies, over 1,200 people: people underestimate how much continuing helps, because creative work feels hard [11] |
| Ideas get worse the longer you keep generating | ❌ | Lucas & Nordgren 2020: people expect this, but ideas stayed as good or got better; a 2024 study also found later ideas more original [12] |
| You can tell from the inside that you're repeating | ❌ | Bilalić, McLeod & Gobet 2008: chess experts said they were looking for a better move while their eyes stayed on the first one [13] |
| Designers copy features of an earlier example, flawed ones included | ✅ | Jansson & Smith 1991, and a 2023 review of 53 experiments [14] |
| A clearly bad first answer is easier to let go of than an okay one | ✅ | Sheridan & Reingold 2013 [13] |

What Claude draws from it (not researched): the feeling of being stuck misleads both ways (it says "out of ideas" too early, and it hides repeating), so the kind has to be read from the attempts themselves: do they share a feature or fail the same way.

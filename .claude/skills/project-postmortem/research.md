# Rebuild what you knew before judging the failure

**Bottom line.** A solo designer should treat old chat transcripts and design docs as witnesses and question them in a fixed order. First, write down what was meant to happen, along with several rival explanations for the failure. Then build a timeline from the artifacts. Then judge each decision using only the turns that came before it, and only after that bring in the outcome. Causes are a set of contributing factors, each tested against the transcript, never one "root cause". To compare several past projects, cut each finished postmortem into one row per problem. Code the rows with a seeded category list that is allowed to grow, then freeze it. Count patterns as "k of n projects". Then check the coding with a second, independent coder. This matters because the main biases (hindsight, outcome bias, sycophancy, an AI favouring its own output) are known to survive warnings. Only structural steps hold them back: hiding the outcome, hiding who said what, and checking against outside evidence. The pieces come from the US Army AAR, Google SRE, the Amazon COE, Toyota's 5 Whys and A3, the London Protocol, CAST, Learning Teams, agile retrospectives, the pre-mortem, and the game-postmortem coding studies of Washburn (155 postmortems) and Politowski (200 postmortems). No source gives a postmortem method for one person, and none covers chat logs between a designer and an AI. So the two recommended procedures at the end are the researchers' own synthesis, built from sourced parts.

**Evidence tags used throughout.** **[verified]** / **[read]**: the researchers read the primary text. **[secondary]**: from a summary, a snippet or someone else's write-up. **[unverified: memory]**: from the researchers' prior knowledge, not confirmed in a source fetched this session, so treat it as a lead. **[inference]**: the researchers' own reasoning, not a source's claim.

## Nine industries share one six-step skeleton and differ only in the "why" step

Almost every method found has the same skeleton: (1) agree what was meant to happen and set the safety rules, (2) rebuild a factual timeline, (3) find the gaps or problems, (4) explain why they happened, (5) commit to owned actions, (6) share the record **[inference, from comparing the methods below]**. The real choice between methods is the causal model in step 4. It can be a single "why" chain (5 Whys, the COE, the A3), a set of contributory-factor categories (the London Protocol), a model of who controls what (CAST), or a study of how normal work actually gets done (Safety-II, Learning Teams). The tables give each method's steps with their inputs and outputs. Where a source gave only outputs, the input is the previous step's output.

**US Army After Action Review (AAR).** It asks four questions: what was expected to happen, what actually happened, what went well and why, and what can be improved and how **[secondary]** ([CEBMA guide](https://tcec.sf.ucdavis.edu/sites/g/files/dgvnsk5301/files/inline-files/CEBMAGuide-to-the-after_action_review_CEBMA.ORG_.pdf)). Another guide words them differently but keeps the shape: planned vs actual, then causes, then fixes ([ADI guide](https://www.adi.org/downloads/After-Action-Review-Guide.pdf)). A formal AAR has a trained facilitator. An informal AAR is run by the people who did the work, right after it, using the same questions, which makes it usable alone **[secondary]** ([CEBMA](https://tcec.sf.ucdavis.edu/sites/g/files/dgvnsk5301/files/inline-files/CEBMAGuide-to-the-after_action_review_CEBMA.ORG_.pdf)). The step list below comes from memory of TC 25-20 (1993), which was not read this session.

| AAR step [unverified: memory] | Input | Output |
|---|---|---|
| Plan | The event and its objectives | Who attends, when and where, the observers |
| Prepare | Observer notes, the site, any recordings | A chronology of key events and the points to discuss |
| Conduct | The participants and the four questions | A shared account, with "sustains" (keep doing) and "improves" (change) |
| Follow up | The improves | Retraining, and changed plans or standard procedures |

**Google SRE postmortem.** It is triggered by criteria set before any incident: user-visible downtime past a threshold, data loss, on-call intervention, resolution time past a threshold, or a monitoring failure. The process is draft, then review, then publish **[verified]** ([SRE book, Postmortem Culture](https://sre.google/sre-book/postmortem-culture/)). Reviewers ask whether key data was collected, whether the impact assessment is complete, whether "the root cause [was] sufficiently deep", whether the action plan is appropriate, and whether the outcome was shared. The template's Lessons Learned section has three parts: what went well, what went wrong, and **"where we got lucky"** **[verified]** ([Example Postmortem](https://sre.google/sre-book/example-postmortem/)).

| SRE step [verified] | Input | Output |
|---|---|---|
| Trigger | Criteria agreed in advance, plus the incident | A decision to write a postmortem |
| Draft | Incident data | A shared document: Summary, Impact, Root Causes, Trigger, Resolution, Detection, Action Items, Lessons Learned, Timeline |
| Review | The draft and the five review criteria | A revised document with open comments closed |
| Publish | The reviewed document | An entry in a team or organisation repository |

**Amazon Correction of Errors (COE).** The damage must be contained before the COE starts. The Summary is written last but placed first, and it must make sense to a reader with no context. Missing metrics are "a red flag". The Five Whys sit under Prevention and stop "when you are confident that you have found the root causes" (plural). Every action item needs a priority, an owner and a due date. The COE has **no what-went-well section** **[verified]** ([AWS blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/mt/why-you-should-develop-a-correction-of-error-coe)).

| COE section [verified] | Input | Output |
|---|---|---|
| Impact | Customer and business data | The impact, measured |
| Timeline | Logs, starting from "the first trigger" | Events in time order |
| Metrics | Monitoring | How the impact is defined, detected and watched |
| Incident questions | The timeline | Answers on detection, diagnosis, mitigation and prevention |
| Five Whys | Each problem | A chain of causes for each problem |
| Action items | The causes | Actions, each with a priority, an owner and a date |
| Summary (written last) | The whole document | A standalone summary for a non-technical reader |

**Toyota 5 Whys and the A3.** The 5 Whys means asking why repeatedly "to get beyond the obvious symptoms". Ohno's example traces a blown fuse back to a missing strainer, and "the specific number five is not the point." The A3 puts the current situation on the left. On the right go the countermeasures, "who will do what when", and the evidence that the issue was actually fixed. LEI calls the A3 a thinking process that can be followed "with or without a physical A3" **[verified]** ([LEI: 5 Whys](https://www.lean.org/lexicon-terms/5-whys/); [LEI: A3](https://www.lean.org/lexicon-terms/a3-report/)). The usual A3 section labels (Background, Current condition, Goal, Root cause, Countermeasures, Plan, Follow-up), and the link to Plan-Do-Check-Act, are **[unverified: memory]** of Shook's *Managing to Learn*.

| 5 Whys stages for a gap from standard [verified] | Input | Output |
|---|---|---|
| Define the problem | The observed gap | A specific problem statement |
| Set a goal | The standard | A target condition |
| Find the root cause | The problem, asking why repeatedly | A causal chain |
| Choose countermeasures | The root cause | Changes to try |
| Check, set standards, follow up | The countermeasures | Proof they worked, and a new standard |

**London Protocol (healthcare).** It asks what happened, how it happened (care delivery problems) and why it happened (contributory factors). A problem counts only if care went beyond safe practice and could have affected the outcome. One problem can have many contributory factors **[secondary]** ([HealthManagement.org](https://healthmanagement.org/pdf/article/the-london-protocol); [cause-x](https://cause-x.squarespace.com/framework-lp)). The 2024 edition renames the problems "care management problems". It also recommends **a few in-depth analyses combined with thematic reviews across many incidents**, which is the same single-case-plus-cross-case split this report recommends **[secondary: abstract snippet]** ([BMJ Qual Saf 2025](https://qualitysafety.bmj.com/content/early/2025/02/22/bmjqs-2024-017987)). The 2004 step list below is **[unverified: memory]**.

| London Protocol 2004 step [unverified: memory] | Input | Output |
|---|---|---|
| Identify the incident and decide to investigate | The incident report | A go/no-go decision and the scope |
| Select the team | | An independent team with mixed skills |
| Organise and gather data | Records, statements, interviews | The evidence set |
| Determine the chronology | The evidence | A timeline (narrative, time-person grid or flow chart) |

| Identify contributory factors | Each problem | Factors sorted into patient, task and technology, individual, team, work environment, organisation, and institutional context |

**STAMP/CAST (Leveson).** CAST aims to find "the limitations of the safety control structure that allowed the loss". In CAST's terms, a controller is any person, organisation or piece of software that makes decisions about the system, and the control structure is the diagram of those controllers and the instructions and feedback that pass between them. It warns against assuming "one 'root cause'". It says "blame is the enemy of safety". It treats the words "should have", "could have" and "if only" as signs of hindsight bias **[secondary quotes of the handbook]** ([MIT OCW notes](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/16-63j-system-safety-spring-2016/d2501c7b1ce13719f38812e6d249dbee_MIT16_63JS16_LecNotes6.pdf); [Entropic Thoughts](https://entropicthoughts.com/root-cause-analysis-youre-doing-it-wrong)). The handbook PDF did not load, so the five steps below are **[unverified: memory]**. Secondary sources confirm only three of them: model the control structure, analyse each component, find systemic factors.

| CAST step [unverified: memory] | Input | Output |
|---|---|---|
| Assemble basic information | The loss, the hazards, the system boundary | A hazard list, the safety constraints, the chain of nearby events |
| Model the control structure | The system | A diagram of the controllers and their control and feedback links |
| Analyse each component | Each controller | Its duties, what it did, what it believed, its context, and **why its actions made sense at the time** |
| Find systemic flaws | All the component analyses | Problems that span components: communication, culture, changes over time, information |
| Improvement program | The flaws | Changes to the structure, with a way to follow them up |

**Safety-II, Learning Teams and the "new view".** Safety-II studies "work as done", meaning the ordinary adjustments that keep things running, not only what failed **[secondary]** ([SHP Online](https://www.shponline.co.uk/culture-and-behaviours/do-we-need-a-new-view-of-safety/amp/)). A patient-safety critique argues that classic root-cause analysis limits learning. It proposes comparing work as imagined with work as done, and looking at what went well **[secondary]** ([PSNet](https://psnet.ahrq.gov/node/866621/psn-pdf)). Learning Teams build in **"soak time"**, a pause between sessions that "predictably allows" members to bring new information **[secondary]** ([Taylor & Francis chapter](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/books/9781315382302/chapters/10.1201/b20559-13)). The Learning Team sequence below is **[unverified: memory]** of Conklin.

|---|---|---|
| Frame the event | The event | An open question, not a cause to confirm |
| Learning session | The people who do the work | A picture of how the work is normally done |
| Soak time | Time to think | New information |

**Agile retrospectives, Kerth, and the pre-mortem.** Derby and Larsen's five stages are: set the stage, gather data (output: "a shared mental model of what happened"), generate insights, decide what to do, and close **[secondary]** ([O'Reilly](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/agile-retrospectives-2nd/9798888650868/f_0010.xhtml)). The Prime Directive asks everyone to assume people did their best with what they knew at the time **[secondary]** ([ThoughtWorks](https://www.thoughtworks.com/en-ca/insights/blog/prime-directive-not-just-star-trek)). Its exact wording, and Kerth's multi-day retrospective, are **[unverified: memory]**. That retrospective includes a timeline built from artifacts, an "emotions seismograph" (a line of how people felt over time), and the question "what still puzzles us". The pre-mortem has a team imagine that a planned project "has just failed" and list the reasons. Its value is "making it safe for dissenters" **[verified: HBR summary]** ([Klein, HBR 2007](https://hbr.org/2007/09/performing-a-project-premortem)). Klein cites Mitchell, Russo and Pennington (1989): imagining the failure had already happened raised the ability to name reasons by 30% ([PDF copy](https://wiki.duraspace.org/download/attachments/104563925/ProjectPremortem.pdf?api=v2)). But Jason Collins points out that the 1989 study did not judge whether the reasons were any good, so the safe reading is **"more reasons", not "30% more accurate"** ([Collins](https://corporate.jasoncollins.blog/premortem)). The pre-mortem steps (write reasons alone for about 2 minutes, go round the room, revise the plan, revisit later) are **[unverified: memory]**.

**UX affinity diagramming.** Following NN/g: write notes alone (5–10 minutes), cluster them into labelled themes, then rank the clusters into action items with owners **[verified]** ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/affinity-diagram/)). NN/g says it "is best created as a team" and gives no guidance for one person.

**What transfers to one person.** Solo-friendly parts are the artifact timeline, the informal AAR questions, a written SRE- or COE-style document, 5 Whys applied to each factor, the London Protocol factor checklist, CAST's "why did it make sense" question and its hindsight-word check, solo clustering, and a solo pre-mortem. Parts that need a team are the Learning Team sessions, round-robins, dot voting, and the independent review that SRE and COE both rely on **[inference]**. No source adapts any of these methods for a solo practitioner. What a solo reviewer loses is other people's views, and the researchers suggest four stand-ins: the artifacts as witnesses, a delay between draft and reread, an outside critic, and the explicit hindsight-word check **[inference]**.

## "Root cause" is a stopping point the analyst chooses, not a fact they find

The methods split on whether a failure has one cause. **5 Whys and the classic A3 aim for a single chain**, asking until "the root cause is reached and eliminated" ([LEI](https://www.lean.org/lexicon-terms/5-whys/)). The COE and SRE use 5 Whys vocabulary but write "root causes" in the plural. SRE's example root cause is itself a combination, "high load and a resource leak", and SRE lists the trigger separately **[verified]** ([SRE example](https://sre.google/sre-book/example-postmortem/)). **CAST, the London Protocol and Safety-II reject a single cause**: "most accidents are actually due to many interacting causes" ([Entropic Thoughts](https://entropicthoughts.com/root-cause-analysis-youre-doing-it-wrong)). The split follows each method's origin. Single-chain methods come from machine faults, where one physical cause is plausible. Multi-factor methods come from accidents that involve people, organisations and machines together, like hospitals and aviation **[inference]**. A failed game design is of the second kind, so the researchers recommend multi-factor framing, with 5 Whys used inside each factor as AWS does **[inference]**. No source compares the two approaches empirically.

John Allspaw gives the sharpest argument: **"Cause is something we construct, not find."** Stopping at one cause satisfies a need for closure, and the analyst decides where the chain starts and stops. Asking "why?" drifts into "who?", while asking "how?" yields stories that contain real data. Calling an action "wrong" depends on knowing the outcome, and asking "why didn't we fail today?" shows that successes also have many contributing conditions **[read in full]** ([Allspaw, The Infinite Hows](https://www.kitchensoap.com/2014/11/14/the-infinite-hows-or-the-dangers-of-the-five-whys/)). Allspaw also quotes Klein's questions for rebuilding a past moment: "What were you seeing?", "What were you expecting to happen?", "Did this situation fit a standard scenario?", and "How did you judge you could influence the course of events?". He adds a check: tell the person's story back to them. For a design postmortem this means a verdict like "the core loop was boring" has probably stopped where closure felt good **[inference]**.

Blame and "what went well" are handled unevenly. Every modern method calls itself blameless. "What went well" is built into the AAR, SRE ("where we got lucky"), Kerth and Safety-II, but **the COE and 5 Whys leave it out**, so they risk throwing away practices that worked **[inference]**. Only CAST names hindsight bias with a concrete check (the counterfactual words). Dekker's equivalent is the local rationality principle: "people do what makes sense to them at the time — given their goals, attentional focus, and knowledge" ([SKYbrary](https://skybrary.aero/index.php/Toolkit:Systems_Thinking_for_Safety/Principle_2._Local_Rationality)). The Prime Directive and SRE handle hindsight by assuming good faith, not with a technique. For a solo reviewer, who has no group to catch their own hindsight, a technique matters more than an assumption **[inference]**.

Two points deserve emphasis. First, the hindsight effect was reported to nearly vanish when the outcome was blamed on chance that nobody could foresee **[secondary: search summary]** ([U. Michigan](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/handle/2027.42/68984?show=full)). That is one more reason to record "where we got lucky" separately. Second, the AI risks stack up. A model that helped write the design will tend to defend its own proposals (self-preference). It will tend to confirm whatever cause the designer names in the prompt (sycophancy). And because it reads the whole transcript, ending included, it is exposed to hindsight. A second pass by the same model makes self-bias worse, not better, so the independent check has to come from outside: real play data, or a context that never saw the earlier verdicts **[inference, from Xu et al.]**.

## Chat transcripts are rare full records of decisions, but silence in them proves nothing

Design rationale research says what matters is the reasons for a decision, the alternatives considered and the trade-offs weighed. Recording that "is still rarely produced in practice" because it is costly ([Cambridge AI EDAM](https://cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/90D195E500BD8688EBE7E8750C817A85/S0890060400001888a.pdf/capturing-and-using-design-rationale.pdf)). Lee lists five ways of capturing it, including "reconstruction" (rebuilding it afterwards) and "record and replay" (recording as it happens) ([WPI report](https://discmaster.textfiles.com/file/11697/ftp.cs.wpi.edu.zip/ftp.cs.wpi.edu/pub/techreports/pdf/99-27.pdf)). A transcript between a designer and an AI is an unusually complete record of the "record and replay" kind. It holds the proposals, the rejected options and the reasons given at the time, which is exactly what is usually lost **[inference]**. Developer–AI chat logs are already a research data source: DevGPT collected 29,778 prompts and answers linked to code ([arXiv 2309.03914](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.03914v2)). But 63–67% of those conversations were single-turn ([HackerNoon](https://hackernoon.com/lite/building-the-devgpt-dataset-for-developer-chatgpt-studies)), and **no study was found that uses chat logs to judge design decisions**.

Transcripts have the known weaknesses of diaries. Keeping one changes behaviour ([Wikipedia: Diary studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diary_studies)), and anything never said is simply absent: playtest feelings, thinking done offline. They are also reactive in a way diaries are not, because the AI's replies shaped the designer's next turns. The log is a record of the process and also a participant in it **[inference]**.

Process tracing turns a transcript into a test of rival stories. It is "the systematic examination of diagnostic evidence selected and analyzed in light of research questions and hypotheses". A **hoop test** checks for evidence that must appear if a story is true: failing it rules the story out, and passing it only keeps the story alive. A **smoking-gun test** checks for evidence that would appear only if the story is true: passing it strongly supports the story, and failing it only weakens it ([BetterEvaluation, after Collier 2011](https://www.betterevaluation.org/tools-resources/understanding-process-tracing)). How strong a test is depends on how common the evidence is (Mahoney) ([Wikipedia](https://Www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Process_tracing)). For a design failure, the rivals might be "the mechanic was never fun", "it was fun but buried under scope" and "the test was wrong". Each rival predicts different timestamps and turns **[inference]**.

The GDC Failure Workshops point at the variable a design postmortem should track. 2D Boy lost six months because they were "more in love with the concept of the game, and not obsessed enough with making the game fun". Shadow Physics was "a great concept but not much fun", and people praised it after watching it, not playing it. Chris Hecker traced tech ratholing and distractions back to one thing: "I was scared of game design" ([Game Developer, GDC 2011](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/console/gdc-2011-game-designers-confront-learn-from-failure)). Washburn found game design went wrong in **22% of 155 postmortems**, mostly from over-ambition and confusing concepts ([PDF](https://thomas-zimmermann.com/publications/files/washburn-icse-2016.pdf)). Shirinian found scope problems in **17 of 24** and an inflexible design or plan in **29%** ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/audio/dissecting-the-postmortem-lessons-learned-from-two-years-of-game-development-self-reportage)). The researchers conclude that a design postmortem should ask "when did we first learn whether the core interaction was fun, and what delayed that?", not "what slipped?". They also conclude that an AI agreeing with a design in chat is evidence of the "praised from watching" kind, weaker than play **[inference]**.

## Cross-project pattern finding is seeded coding that grows until it stops changing

Every cross-case method found follows the same sequence: pick documents, cut them into units, code the units with a list that grows until new codes stop appearing (saturation), check that coders agree, then count and compare **[read; synthesis is inference]**. The two game studies are the direct precedent.

| Washburn et al. 2016 step [read] | Input | Output |
|---|---|---|
| Corpus | 215 Gamasutra postmortems | 155 usable postmortems (inclusion rule: a game postmortem with right and wrong sections) |
| Seed | Shirinian's analysis of 24 postmortems | 12 starting categories |
| Iterate (3 weeks: 3, 10, 15 postmortems per coder) | The seed list plus new postmortems; earlier ones recoded | 22 categories, frozen after 60 postmortems |
| Bulk code | The frozen list plus about 95 remaining postmortems | Coded items, with weekly alignment talks and no agreement statistic |
| Count | Coded items | % of postmortems mentioning each category as right or wrong |
| Compare subgroups | Metadata (team size, publisher, platform) | Raw % differences, no significance tests (e.g. obstacles went wrong for 50% of small teams vs 26% of large) |
| Report | All of the above | Each category with a description, an example quote and a takeaway, plus the raw data released |
([Washburn et al. 2016](https://thomas-zimmermann.com/publications/files/washburn-icse-2016.pdf))

| Politowski et al. 2020/2021 step [read] | Input | Output |
|---|---|---|
| Sample | 20 random postmortems per author, some picked by hand to balance years | A batch |

The generic qualitative methods give the vocabulary for these steps. Braun and Clarke's **reflexive thematic analysis** has six phases: familiarisation notes, systematic coding, generating initial themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, writing up. A theme is "a pattern of shared meaning, cohering around a central concept". A one-word bucket like "Design" is a topic summary, which is really a code, and themes "must be analytic outputs, not inputs". Reflexive TA does not need a research team and rejects inter-rater reliability. Codebook TA (framework and template analysis) and coding-reliability TA fix their categories early **[read]** ([Braun & Clarke 2021](https://www.sfu.ca/~palys/Braun&Clarke-2021-ReflexiveThematicAnalysis.pdf)). The phase-by-phase inputs and outputs are the researchers' mapping **[inference]**: raw documents become familiarisation notes, then coded extracts, then candidate themes, then a revised theme map, then defined themes, then the report.

| Method | Steps (input to output) | Tag |
|---|---|---|
| Grounded theory, Charmaz version | Initial coding, close to the data, often line by line → focused coding (the most frequent or significant codes, applied to more data) → theoretical coding, with memos raising codes into categories | [secondary] ([guide](https://www.groundedtheoryonline.com/which-grounded-theory/)) |
| Grounded theory, Strauss & Corbin version | Open coding → axial coding (relating categories through conditions, actions and consequences) → selective coding around a core category. Criticised for forcing data into predefined relations | [secondary] |
| Card sort | Open sort (sorters make the groups) to build a taxonomy, then closed sort (fixed groups) to validate it | [secondary] ([GOV.UK](https://userresearch.blog.gov.uk/2018/03/23/how-we-refined-our-approach-to-card-sorting/)) |
| KJ / affinity method | One idea per card → silent grouping → header card per group → arrows for relations. Gives no ranking by itself | [secondary] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affinity_diagram)) |
| Template analysis (King) | A few themes set in advance → code a subset → build a hierarchical template → apply it and revise until stable | **[unverified]** |
| ASRS (aviation) | Each report gets a fixed hierarchical code: Anomaly, Primary Problem, Contributing Factors, Detector, Human Factors | [snippet] ([ASRS taxonomy](https://asrs.arc.nasa.gov/docs/dbol/ASRS_CodingTaxonomy.pdf)) |
| FAIL (LLM pipeline over news) | Collect articles → classify relevance → merge articles about the same incident → analyse each against existing taxonomies. Gave 2,457 failures from 137,427 articles and found high recurrence | [abstract] ([arXiv 2406.08221](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08221v2)) |

Two warnings apply to this table. Stol, Ralph and Fitzgerald found that only 16 of 98 software-engineering articles mentioning grounded theory described their procedures in detail, and they warn against "method slurring" ([Stol et al. 2016](https://cora.ucc.ie/handle/10468/7041)). The researchers read the game studies as using grounded theory techniques, not full grounded theory **[inference]**. And reflexive TA is the better fit when the question is why patterns recur, while seeded coding fits how often they recur **[inference]**.

**Seed or build from the data?** All the game studies did both: seed from earlier work, let new codes in during a pilot, and freeze once nothing new appears. Pure fixed lists (ASRS, closed sorts) fit when results must be comparable over years. Pure bottom-up coding fits a new domain. Open sorts come first, because closed sorts "were not useful" before good categories existed ([GOV.UK](https://userresearch.blog.gov.uk/2018/03/23/how-we-refined-our-approach-to-card-sorting/)). Seeding risks categories like "Design" that count well but explain little, so a finer second pass is needed **[inference]**.

**Counting.** You can count by document ("% of postmortems that mention X", so one long postmortem can't dominate) or by item ("% of all problems"), then split by metadata. Precedent corpora had 155–200 documents, and their category lists saturated at 60 and about 140 documents. Neither ran significance tests. Self-reported counts measure "what authors chose to write", not how often failures really happened **[inference]**. No source gives a sample-size rule. A solo designer with a handful of projects is far below these numbers, so report "k of n projects", read the patterns qualitatively, and treat the item level (about 5 problems per project) as the main analysis unit **[inference]**.

**One coded row.** A Politowski record holds an ID, the postmortem title, year, source URL, game name, platform, genre, mode, group, type, sub-type and a verbatim quote ([Politowski 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.00491)). ASRS adds a primary problem plus contributing factors and a detector. NASA's lessons-learned records hold a driving event (what, where, when), the lessons and recommendations ([KSC form](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/kdp-ksc-p-2393-revd-scof.pdf?emrc=6ad8b360ba94f)). The researchers merged these into one template **[inference]**: id, source and locator, verbatim quote, polarity, group, type, sub-type, contributing factor, consequence, proposed fix, coder, date, memo, confidence.

**Reliability for one coder.** Campbell et al. describe negotiated agreement. You first standardise the text units ("unitization"), then refine the coding scheme until two coders agree. They reached 96–97%, meant for the case where "a single knowledgeable coder will code all the transcripts" afterwards **[abstract]** ([Campbell et al. 2013](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0049124113500475)). Cohen's kappa measures agreement between two coders, corrected for agreement by chance. LLMs used as second coders agree with humans unevenly. GPT-3 reached kappa 0.38–0.61 against experts ([Xiao et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.10548)). GPT-4 reached about 0.46 ([QualiGPT](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.14925)). A step-by-step prompt reached 0.744 ([arXiv 2507.14384](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.14384v1)). GPT-4 reached at least 0.79 on only 3 of 9 codes, did worst on interpretive (latent) codes, and invented codes that were not in the codebook ([arXiv 2401.15170](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.15170v1)). The recommended split is that the LLM applies the codebook (deductive coding) while the human builds new codes from the data (inductive coding) and interprets. Agreement with a model shows that the codebook is clear to a model, not that the coding is true **[inference]**. Two other single-coder checks are **[unverified this session]**: re-coding a 10–20% sample after a gap of weeks, and keeping an audit trail of codebook versions and decisions. The Landis and Koch kappa bands are also **[unverified]**.

## Conclusion

The research changes where the effort goes. The biases that spoil postmortems survive good intentions. Fischhoff's warned subjects, Baron and Hershey's subjects who disowned the outcome, and assistants that grow more sycophantic over many turns all show this. So a solo postmortem succeeds or fails on its order of steps. The outcome has to stay hidden while each decision is judged. The rival explanations have to be written before anyone reads the evidence. The check has to come from something that took no part in the sessions. Transcripts make this possible to an unusual degree, because they let you stop reading at any decision. But they also hold the AI's own voice, so the AI that helped design a failed game is the analyst most likely to excuse it, and it should be the coder whose work gets checked, not the judge.

For comparing projects, the honest limit is scale. Published pattern studies needed 60–140 documents before their category lists settled. A solo designer has a handful of projects, so the cross-project result is a register of tested patterns with named counter-cases, not a frequency table. The variable that most deserves a column in that register is the one the GDC failure talks kept returning to: how long each project ran before its core interaction was actually played **[inference]**. No source has studied design postmortems built from conversations between a designer and an AI, so this method is untested and should itself be reviewed after its first use.

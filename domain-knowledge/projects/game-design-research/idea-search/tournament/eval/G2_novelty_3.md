# Novelty batch 3 (18 ideas)

### G203 Foam Bulkhead
- Rule (one sentence): Foam sprayed into the hull swells to three times its size over four seconds,
  shoving anything soft out of the way, then sets rock-hard around whatever is still inside it.
- Hub (the one shared thing everyone uses): the hull, with its water and its foam.
- Coupling (how my best move depends on what a friend just did; who does which different action):
  - A breach's water jet washes soft foam away, so one player blocks the jet with a hatch board while
    another sprays around them.
  - The blocker must pull out just before the foam sets, or be set in. A trapped player is freed only
    when a friend chips them out with a pick.
  - The sprayer's timing depends on when the blocker is braced, and the blocker's pull-out depends on
    how much foam was just sprayed.
  - Swelling foam from one patch shoves friends working on the next one.
- Fantasy (who you are, what you're doing): the crew of a sinking paddle steamer patching holes to
  reach the shore.
- Look (one line): a dark wooden hull, yellow foam bulging and crusting over, crew stuck in it up to
  the neck.
- At 3 / at 6:
  - At 3: blocker, sprayer, and a chipper who also bails.
  - At 6: two blocker+sprayer pairs, a chipper and a bailer.
- Hidden: none.
- A failed run (what happens, who is blamed): the blocker is set into the foam, nobody is free to
  block the next breach, and the hold floods. The foam that trapped them is tinted with the sprayer's
  colour.
- Tail (what makes round 50 different from round 2, with no hand-made content):
  - New breaches open where the water pushes hardest.
  - The hard foam already placed changes the hull's weight and trim, so breaches open in new places
    every run, and each hull is the crew's own patchwork.
- Search (swap-in for the magnet idea): co-op ship-patching games exist (Grim Repair, Piece of Ship,
  Barotrauma), but none uses foam that swells, shoves and sets with people inside (N ~).

### G206 Crib the House
- Rule (one sentence): A corner of the house rises one block only when one player heaves its
  jack and a different player slides a block under it at the top of the heave. If the lifter lets
  go first, the corner drops back.
- Hub (the one shared thing everyone uses): the house, standing on one timber stack per corner.
- Coupling (how my best move depends on what a friend just did; who does which different action):
  - The lifter picks when to heave and holds for at most ~1.5 s.
  - The slider picks when to shove the block in, and needs a block in hand.
  - Blocks are planks floating past in the flood. A third player hooks them out.
  - Neighbouring corners may differ by at most 2 blocks, so the corner a friend just raised
    decides which corner I must raise next.
  - Lift, slide and hook rotate as players move round the house.
- Fantasy (who you are, what you're doing): a farm family jacking the farmhouse up on timber
  stacks while the river floods.
- Look (one line): a tilted wooden house on wobbling stacks of timber, with brown water rising.
- At 3 / at 6:
  - At 3: a triangle hut. One player lifts, one slides and one hooks planks, and the jobs rotate
    every heave.
  - At 6: a hexagon house. Up to three lift-and-slide pairs work at once. Planks are scarce, so one
    or two players hook, and the flood rises faster.
- Hidden: none.
- A failed run (what happens, who is blamed): a corner crashes down, the house tips past 2 blocks
  and slides off its stacks into the water. Blame is visible at that corner:
  - the block lies on the mud beside the stack: the slider missed;
  - the block was still in the slider's hands when the corner fell: the lifter let go early.
- Tail (what makes round 50 different from round 2, with no hand-made content): each round
  generates the ground under each corner (rock, or mud that sinks a stack one block every N
  seconds), the plank stream (lengths and timing), the house outline for the crew size and the
  flood rate. Which corners sink, and when, is new every round.

### G209 Teeterboard Troupe
- Rule (one sentence): The player on the low end of a teeterboard flies only if they jump in the
  same instant another player lands on the high end. The lander then sits at the low end waiting
  for the next lander, so the troupe keeps juggling itself while water fills the quarry.
- Hub (the one shared thing everyone uses): the teeterboards on the quarry ledges.
- Coupling (how my best move depends on what a friend just did; who does which different action):
  - The lander steers in the air and picks the board. The sitter picks the jump moment: jump early
    and you get a low hop; jump late and you get nothing.
  - Flyers ring gongs on the walls at the top of their arc.
  - With several players in the air, who lands where is a live negotiation.
- Fantasy (who you are, what you're doing): a circus troupe trapped in a flooding quarry, ringing
  gongs to call for help.
- Look (one line): striped costumes against grey stone, and big splashes.
- At 3 / at 6:
  - At 3: 2 boards, with someone always in the air.
  - At 6: 3 or 4 boards, two or three players in the air at once, and possible mid-air crashes.
- Hidden: none.
- A failed run (what happens, who is blamed): a flyer falls short into the water. Either the
  sitter jumped early (a visibly low arc) or the lander came down off the end of the board. The
  splashed player swims slowly to a ladder, the chain runs short of players, and the water reaches
  the top board before the gongs are rung. The clock is the rising water, which drowns the lowest
  boards first.
- Tail (what makes round 50 different from round 2, with no hand-made content): generated board
  layouts on the ledges (heights and gaps), gong positions and the water rate.

### G212 Jelly Kitchen
- Rule (one sentence): Every move a player makes on the jelly floor sends out a visible ripple; ripples
  add up or cancel where they meet, and anything sitting on a high crest tips over.
- Hub (the one shared thing everyone uses): the jelly floor (one wave field).
- Coupling (how my best move depends on what a friend just did; who does which different action):
  - The kitchen is a chain: chop → cook → plate → run. Each station has one delicate moment (pouring,
    stacking, carrying a full tray) that needs calm floor under it.
  - A friend's step reaches me as a ripple a second later. I can wait it out, step aside, or stomp in
    time to cancel it (a crest meeting a trough). The cancelling stomp sends its own ripple on to
    someone else.
  - Different jobs, and the chain stops if one station stops.
- Fantasy (who you are, what you're doing): cooks in a restaurant built on a giant dessert, getting
  orders out during the dinner rush.
- Look (one line): a glossy pastel jelly floor with rings in each cook's colour spreading from their
  feet.
- At 3 / at 6:
  - At 3: chop+cook, plate, run. Each person walks more, so each makes more ripples.
  - At 6: two cooks, two platers, a runner and a dishwasher. The floor gets busy, and cancelling
    ripples becomes part of everyone's job.
- Hidden: none.
- A failed run (what happens, who is blamed): a stack of plates tips over. The crest that tipped it
  shows the colour of the cook whose step made it, or whose cancelling stomp came half a beat late.
  Orders time out.
- Tail (what makes round 50 different from round 2, with no hand-made content):
  - Ripples from 3–6 people meet in new patterns every service.
  - The orders are drawn at random from a list of ≤ 20 dishes, so the busy stations move around.
- Search: Jelly Express (co-op jelly box balancing) and The Floor is Jelly (single-player). No
  ripple-interference rule found.

### G215 Floe Haul
- Rule (one sentence): Each player rides their own drifting ice floe, and any two can throw a rope across and haul their floes together, which also drags every floe roped to either of them.

- Coupling (how my best move depends on what a friend just did; who does which different action): I want to pull Ana's floe out of an iceberg's path, but she is still roped to Cal. My haul drags Cal too, unless Cal cuts loose or hauls the other way at the same moment. A thrown rope needs a catcher with free hands. The actions are throw, catch, haul, cut, and hop across when two floes touch.
- Fantasy (who you are, what you're doing): a polar camp whose ice broke up overnight, hauling itself back together and over to the ship before the current carries everyone past it.
- Look (one line): white floes on black water, red ropes snapping taut, a ship's lights far off.
- At 3 / at 6: each player carries one rope, so there are 3 ropes at 3 and 6 at 6. At 6, the web is big enough that one haul can tug three people.
- Hidden (what, how it's uncovered, why anyone hides it) or "none": none.
- A failed run (what happens, who is blamed): Dee's floe hit the iceberg. Cal hauled toward Ben while Dee was hauling away, and the rope between them snapped tight and dragged her sideways. Everyone saw the haul.
- Tail (what makes round 50 different from round 2, with no hand-made content): the floe field, the currents and the cracks are generated, floes split mid-round, and the rope web is physics, so every round is a new tangle.

### G218 Shove Crew
- Rule (one sentence): No one can walk; you can only shove a friend away from you, so the crew crosses the board by pushing each other.
- Hub (the one shared thing everyone uses): the players' bodies on one board.
- Coupling (how my best move depends on what a friend just did; who does which different action): where I can go depends entirely on who stands behind me and when they shove. My best shove depends on where the last shove just left each friend. At any moment one player lines up, one shoves, and one is the one being moved.
- Fantasy (who you are, what you're doing): clumsy penguins escaping melting ice by shoving each other across it.
- Look (one line): flat ice floes from above, cracks spreading, and round penguins skidding in a line.
- At 3 / at 6: at 3 every shove matters and someone always ends up stuck behind. At 6 it is a crowded, chaotic billiards game, with more angles and more people in the way.
- Hidden (what, how it's uncovered, why anyone hides it) or "none": none.
- A failed run (what happens, who is blamed): a penguin skids into the water. The one who shoved is seen doing it, at the angle they chose.

### G221 Hump Yard
- Rule (one sentence): Freight cars roll one by one off a hill into a fan of sidings, and players throw the switches to send each car to its colour's track and ride the cars to brake them by hand so they couple gently.
- Hub (the one shared thing everyone uses): the switch ladder below the hill.
- Coupling (how my best move depends on what a friend just did; who does which different action): A switch can be thrown only in the gap between two cars, and the gap depends on how hard the rider ahead is braking. A rider who brakes early to avoid a hard coupling shrinks the gap for the switch tender behind. A late tender forces the rider of the next car to brake hard to buy time. Riders run back up the hill after each car, so who rides which car is called out on the fly. 2 extra rules: heavy cars roll faster, and a full siding forces a reroute.
- Fantasy (who you are, what you're doing): the night shift at a 1950s freight yard, brakemen and switchmen with lanterns.
- Look (one line): sodium lights, steam, wet rails, boxcars painted by destination.
- At 3 / at 6: 3 = 1 tender running between 3 switch stands, 2 riders, 4 sidings. 6 = 2–3 tenders, 3–4 riders, 7 sidings. Your job is wherever you stand.
- Hidden (what, how it's uncovered, why anyone hides it) or "none": none (car weight is chalked on the side).
- A failed run (what happens, who is blamed): a switch thrown under a car derails it (the tender, seen at the lever); a car slams into the cut at speed and its cargo bursts (its rider, seen jumping off); a car stops short and fouls the ladder (its rider braked too much). Three wrecks end the shift.
- Tail (what makes round 50 different from round 2, with no hand-made content): a generated train each shift (car order, destinations, weights). Sidings fill as the night goes on, so roll distances shrink and change. The hill engine pushes faster over the shift.

### G224 Salt Cross

  - If either sweeps while the other is still on, salt grows in the wrong place and walls off the

  - At odd sizes the extra player takes brine or heat.

### G227 Megalith Haul
- Rule (one sentence): A huge stone slides only over logs, and each log that rolls out behind it must be carried round and laid in front before the stone reaches bare ground.
- Hub (the one shared thing everyone uses): the stone on its row of rollers.
- Coupling (how my best move depends on what a friend just did; who does which different action): Rope players pull the stone and set its pace. Runners grab the log freed at the back, run it forward and lay it, and the angle they lay it at turns the stone. As a runner I lay my log to fix or continue the turn the last runner made. As a rope player I slow down when no runner is coming with a log. On a downhill the stone rolls by itself, and rope players must switch to the back ropes and hold it, so the jobs flip with the ground. 2 extra rules: rope pull, log angle steers.
- Fantasy (who you are, what you're doing): a stone-age village moving a standing stone to the henge before the solstice sunrise.
- Look (one line): pale chalk hills at dawn, a grey slab riding on pale logs, hide ropes, people in wool.
- At 3 / at 6: 3 = 1 on the rope, 2 runners, slow stone, short path. 6 = 3 rope, 3 runners, faster stone, longer path. Anyone can drop a rope and become a runner.
- Hidden (what, how it's uncovered, why anyone hides it) or "none": none.
- A failed run (what happens, who is blamed): the stone's front edge passes the last log and digs in, or tips off a skewed log into a ditch. The gap is where one runner was due, still holding their log, or the rope player who kept pulling; everyone sees it.
- Tail (what makes round 50 different from round 2, with no hand-made content): generated ground each haul (slopes, mud where logs sink, boulders and ditches to steer round). Logs split after a few passes, so supply varies. A skewed log turns the stone, so no two hauls take the same line.

### G230 Log Jam
- Rule (one sentence): Players stand on a jam of floating logs above a mill dam and pry the logs loose with poles before the water tops the dam, and every log you push rolls or shifts the logs your friends are standing on.
- Hub (the one shared thing everyone uses): the floating log pile (one physics pile).
- Coupling (how my best move depends on what a friend just did; who does which different action): before I push, I check who stands on that log or its neighbours, and I call it. A player can hook a pole into a log to hold it still, so a friend can cross it or push from it. The key log holding the jam (it bows and creaks) moves only when two players push it from different sides at the same moment. When the jam breaks, everyone runs for the bank over rolling logs, and the order matters. Actions: push, pin a log, cross.
- Fantasy (who you are, what you're doing): 1890s river drivers breaking a log jam in the spring flood.
- Look (one line): brown spray, a tangle of pine logs, drivers in red wool with long pike poles.
- At 3 / at 6: 3 = a small jam with one key log: one player pins while two push. 6 = a bigger jam with two key logs that must go in order, and more bodies rolling logs under each other. Each round's jam is a generated pile on one river.
- Hidden (what, how it's uncovered, why anyone hides it) or "none": none (logs under strain bow and creak).
- A failed run (what happens, who is blamed): a player is rolled into the water and the logs close over them. The player who pushed that log is standing on the next one with their pole still out. Or the water tops the dam because the two key-log pushers didn't push together.

### G236 Hot Rivets
- Rule (one sentence): A rivet sets only if one player throws it, glowing, into a hole in the
  steel frame and another player's hammer comes down on it in the same instant, while it still
  glows (~2 s).
- Hub (the one shared thing everyone uses): the steel frame high above the street.
- Coupling (how my best move depends on what a friend just did; who does which different action):
  - Players stand in a ring of joints. Each hammers what the previous player throws and throws to
    the next player's joint.
  - The thrower picks the arc and the moment, and the wind bends the throw.
  - The hammer swing takes ~0.6 s, so the hammerer must start it before the rivet lands, on a call.
  - A hammer on an empty hole rings the beam and shakes the frame, which spoils the neighbours'
    next throws.
- Fantasy (who you are, what you're doing): a 1930s riveting gang on a skyscraper, racing a
  rising wind.
- Look (one line): orange dots arcing against a grey sky, with the city far below.
- At 3 / at 6: a ring of 3 or 6 joints, and everyone both throws and hammers. One player cannot
  throw and swing at once, so the crew has to fall into a rhythm. At 6 there are more rivets in
  the air and more shaking.
- Hidden: none.
- A failed run (what happens, who is blamed): a rivet drops to the street. A wide throw is the
  thrower's fault. A rivet left sitting blue in its hole means the hammerer was late. Too many cold
  joints and the frame sways until a beam falls at that joint. The clock is the cooling rivets and
  wind that rises every minute.
- Tail (what makes round 50 different from round 2, with no hand-made content): each round
  generates the frame's shape (distances, heights and angles between joints) and the wind's gust
  pattern, and the frame grows taller during a run.

### G239 On Air

Where I should stand depends on who is live and where their frame will lose the action. The live seat moves around the crew with every handoff, so nobody directs for long. 1 extra rule: crowds and obstacles block sight lines.

- Hidden (what, how it's uncovered, why anyone hides it) or "none": none (all camera views are shown as small tiles).
- A failed run (what happens, who is blamed): dead air, with an empty plaza on the broadcast and a number in the corner. - Tail (what makes round 50 different from round 2, with no hand-made content): the bull is one simple system (charges, swerves, stops at random), so its path never repeats. Venues are 2–3 layouts with crowds and carts placed at random.

### G254 Misdirection
- Rule (one sentence): The audience always looks at the biggest motion on stage, and a secret move only works if nobody in the audience is looking at it.
- Hub (the one shared thing everyone uses): the audience's gaze, one simple system shown as heads turning.

A flourisher must draw eyes away from wherever the sneak goes next, and a flourish too close to the sneak drags eyes onto them. 1 extra rule: flourish fatigue.
- Fantasy (who you are, what you're doing): a travelling magic troupe on a Victorian music-hall stage.
- Look (one line): velvet curtains, limelight, a sea of top hats whose heads visibly swivel.
- At 3 / at 6: 3 = one trick at a time, roles swap between tricks. 6 = two tricks at once on opposite sides of the stage, so one trick's flourish can expose the other trick's sneak.
- Hidden (what, how it's uncovered, why anyone hides it) or "none": none between players (secret moves are hidden only from the audience, and players see the gaze).
- A failed run (what happens, who is blamed): a head snaps to the sneak mid-move, gasps and points; the trick is blown. Seen in the moment: the sneak moved while eyes were near, or a flourisher stopped too early or flourished too close. Three blown tricks and the troupe is booed off.
- Tail (what makes round 50 different from round 2, with no hand-made content): the gaze tires of repeated flourishes, so the crew must vary them. Each night's act is drawn from a short list of about 15 tricks (which prop or person must end where) on 2–3 stage layouts.

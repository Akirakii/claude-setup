# Design theory: verbs, emergence, player interaction and content cost

Tags: **[read]** = I fetched and read that page this session (strongest). **[source]** = from a search snippet or secondary summary with a link, page not read in full (verify before quoting). **[opinion]** = my inference, not from a source. Researched 2026-11-09. Memory-only recall is kept out of the findings and listed under Gaps.

Working frame used below [opinion]: a verb stays fun while it keeps producing new patterns to learn (Koster). Three things can supply those new patterns: (1) **authored content** (levels, enemies, items), which costs money per unit and gets used up; (2) **rule combinations** (emergence), which are cheap per session but cost design and tuning time; (3) **other minds** (players reading, fooling and helping each other), which cost almost nothing per session but only work if the verbs let players affect and read each other under uncertainty. In the user's terms, "high coupling between players" means source (3), and "low content" means avoiding a heavy dependence on source (1).

## 1. Juul: games of emergence vs games of progression (2002 paper; Half-Real)

### Takeaway
Juul's emergence games are a few rules that combine into many variations. They are replayable and get strategy guides. Progression games are a designer-set sequence of challenges. They have walkthroughs, are hardly replayable, and need content to go on. Only progression depends on authored content. Juul classes Counter-Strike-style team play as emergence.

### Cited Findings
- Emergence is defined as "simple rules combining, leading to variation", and progression as "serially introduced challenges". — [read] [Juul 2002, The Open and the Closed](https://www.jesperjuul.net/text/openandtheclosed.html)
- Emergence: "a game is specified as a small number of rules that combine and yield large numbers of game variations". Progression: "the player has to perform a predefined set of actions in order to complete the game." — [read] [Juul 2002](https://www.jesperjuul.net/text/openandtheclosed.html)
- The variation in emergence "is not just random or supplied by the user, but is a non-obvious consequence of the rules of a game." — [read] [Juul 2002](https://www.jesperjuul.net/text/openandtheclosed.html)
- In progression, "the designer controls the sequence of events", and "all of the options and solutions have been explicitly described in the game rules". Progression structures are "heavily pre-controlled by the game". — [read] [Juul 2002](https://www.jesperjuul.net/text/openandtheclosed.html)
- Replay: "Emergence games tend to be replayable and tend to foster tournaments and strategy guides." For progression, "their replayability is subsequently very low". — [read] [Juul 2002](https://www.jesperjuul.net/text/openandtheclosed.html)
- "Progression games have walkthroughs, specifying all the actions needed to complete the game." "Emergence games have strategy guides: rules of thumb, general tricks." — [read] [Juul 2002](https://www.jesperjuul.net/text/openandtheclosed.html)
- Examples: Counter-Strike games "almost always lead to fights between the two teams", Monopoly always ends in bankruptcy, and Quake II rocket-jumping and Deus Ex mine-climbing are given as emergence. Branching narratives are "probably the purest example" of progression. EverQuest is "a game of emergence, with embedded progression structures". — [read] [Juul 2002](https://www.jesperjuul.net/text/openandtheclosed.html)
- On multiplayer: "the actual game played has input from a lot of different sources in addition to the game designers". In EverQuest, though, "the social aspects of the world are characterised using progression" (group quests, team-only monsters). — [read] [Juul 2002](https://www.jesperjuul.net/text/openandtheclosed.html)

### Inferences
- [opinion] Juul's split matches the user's formula closely. Content-heavy games are progression games; their cost scales with hours of play because each challenge is used once (walkthrough = consumed). Emergence games pay once for rules, and other players' inputs are one of the "sources in addition to the game designers".
- [opinion] Juul's EverQuest point is a warning. Social play can itself be built as progression: scripted group gates such as "monster needs 3 people". That gives player coupling, but it is coupling through content, so each new gate costs content again. Cheap coupling has to come from rules (emergence), not from authored group checks.
- [opinion] A strategy guide exists for emergence games because general tricks transfer between sessions. A friendslop game whose only "guide" is a walkthrough (do X at node Y) has fallen into progression.

### Gaps
- I did not fetch *Half-Real* (MIT Press 2005), so its chapter text on emergence and progression is unverified here. From memory only: Half-Real calls emergence "the primordial game structure" and progression a newer structure that came into video games with adventure games. Check this against the book (ch. 3) before citing it.
- The 2002 paper does not quantify rule counts or content volume (confirmed [read]).

## 2. Elias, Garfield and Gutschera, *Characteristics of Games* (MIT Press 2012); Garfield on interaction

### Takeaway
The book treats interaction as a named characteristic, next to player elimination, politics, kingmaking and teamwork. It splits multiplayer games into low-interaction "races" and high-interaction "brawls", and notes that politics and kingmaking need three or more sides plus targeted interaction. I could not verify the exact passages on depth, solvability or "other players as the source of variety".

### Cited Findings
- A table of contents in a retail listing shows these chapters: "Characteristic: Player Elimination" (p. 38), "Interactivity" (p. 44), "Politics" (p. 48), "Kingmaking" (p. 56) and "Teamwork" (p. 61). — [source] retail TOC surfaced in search, e.g. [Tenlong listing](https://www.tenlong.com.tw/products/9780262542692)
- A review says the book ranges from "virtually no interactivity between opponents", as in track races, to high-interaction games where players affect one another, and that such games often hide a "chip taking" dynamic. — [source] [Entrogames review](https://entrogames.substack.com/p/book-review-characteristics-of-games-george-skaff-elias-richard-garfield-k-robert-gutschera) (snippet; the original URL returned 404 on fetch)
- A board-game blog reports that the authors use a "token taking game" to show the effects of targeted player interaction, and call such games "political games". — [source] [Board Game Snob](https://boardgamesnob.com/2016/12/12/player-interaction-in-multiplayer-games/)
- Search summaries of book excerpts say the following. The book divides multiplayer games into races and brawls. Races tend toward logical elimination and low interaction, while brawls tend toward high interaction, politics and kingmaking. Kingmaking depends on targeted interaction plus logically eliminated players. Politics and kingmaking only arise with three or more sides. Nim is "essentially impossible for a new player to decipher" but fully solved for experts, and solvable games such as Nim or tic-tac-toe have poor heuristics in the sense of being enjoyable. — [source, weak] search-engine summary of excerpts whose pages appear to be unofficial PDF copies (not linked). Verify against the [MIT Press edition](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262542692/characteristics-of-games/).
- A blogger's paraphrase: "Any game where you can choose another player and help or harm them will have some degree of politics." "A good game has ways for new players to quickly pick up some basic strategies" (heuristics). "Randomness is a catch up mechanic." — [read] [Online Dungeon Master tag page](https://onlinedungeonmaster.com/tag/characteristics-of-games/) (the blogger's words, not the book's)
- Garfield (2012 interview): more luck can leave less room for skill to show, though skill still matters, and even Scrabble has "quite a bit of luck". — [source] [BGG interview thread](https://boardgamegeek.com/thread/843509/interview-i-conducted-with-richard-garfield-april) (snippet; the fetch returned 403)

### Inferences
- [opinion] The race/brawl split is a direct test of player coupling. A race, where everyone optimises their own track, is "multiplayer solitaire": its depth must come from rules or content. A brawl gets depth from opponents, but brings politics and kingmaking with it. For co-op friendslop, the useful middle is targeted interaction without elimination: players choose whom to help or hinder, but nobody can be locked out.
- [opinion] Solvability ties to content cost. A solved or solvable verb set (Nim) is dead once known, so a solo-content game needs new content to stay unsolved. Another mind stops a verb set from being solved, because the best move depends on what that person will do.

### Gaps
- I found no verified passage stating "the other players are the source of variety". Do not attribute that phrase to the book.
- From memory only, unverified: the book frames depth as a "skill ladder" (how many distinct skill levels a game supports), and lists other players' choices and hidden information as sources of uncertainty next to dice. Check these against the book before citing.
- Garfield's own statements on interaction (outside the book) were not reachable: the BGG page returned 403. His GDC talks were not searched.

## 3. Björk and Holopainen, *Patterns in Game Design* (2004): hidden information, bluffing, social interaction

### Takeaway
In the pattern catalogue, bluffing is not a verb you add. It emerges from **asymmetric information** plus a way to give **indirect information**. It creates tension and opens the door to betrayal, and it shapes "Social Interaction". Symmetric, direct information and pure luck work against it.

### Cited Findings
- The book groups its patterns into eleven major groups, including "social interaction", "replay value" and "game session". — [source] [arXiv literature review](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1603.06996)
- Bluffing is defined as: "Players have a possibility to convey false information to other players in order to benefit from the situation." — [read] [Bluffing pattern (VU Amsterdam mirror of the catalogue)](https://math.vu.nl/~eliens/media/pattern-bluffing.html)
- Requirements: "the game should have Asymmetric Information together with means of players giving each other Indirect Information about game components". "Games that have Symmetric Information as well as Direct Information about game elements limit the possibilities". — [read] [Bluffing pattern](https://math.vu.nl/~eliens/media/pattern-bluffing.html)
- Consequences: "creates uncertainty about results and thereby Tension", and "in most cases leads to at least a possibility of Betrayal." — [read] [Bluffing pattern](https://math.vu.nl/~eliens/media/pattern-bluffing.html)
- Relations:
  - instantiates Risk/Reward, Betrayal and Tension;
  - modulates Bidding, Trading, Emotional Immersion, Alarms and Social Interaction;
  - is instantiated by Indirect Information, Social Interaction, Negotiation, Betting and Asymmetric Information;
  - is modulated by Direct and Symmetric Information;
  - potentially conflicts with Symmetric Information and Luck.

  Bluffing is "possible in almost all cases of Negotiation". — [read] [Bluffing pattern](https://math.vu.nl/~eliens/media/pattern-bluffing.html)
- The mirror page does not itself credit Björk and Holopainen; the link to the book comes from search context. — [read] (same page)
- Related patterns on the mirror include Indirect Information, Uncertainty of Information and Symmetric Information. — [source] [Indirect Information](https://math.vu.nl/~eliens/media/pattern-indirectinformation.html), [Uncertainty of Information](https://math.vu.nl/~eliens/demo/pattern-uncertaintyofinformation.html)

### Inferences
- [opinion] This is the cheapest depth recipe in the literature for the user's formula. Give players different knowledge (asymmetric info) and a lossy channel to share it (indirect info: voice, gestures, actions others can watch). The interesting play then comes from players, not content. One rule creates an endless supply of new situations.
- [opinion] "Conflicts with Luck" matters. If dice decide everything, reading the other person stops mattering. Keep randomness for setup (who knows what), not for outcomes.

### Gaps
- The canonical wiki (gameplaydesignpatterns.org, now redirecting to gdp.cse.chalmers.se) returned 404 for "Imperfect Information". I could not read that pattern's own text or the "Social Interaction" pattern page.

## 4. Verb-based design: Anthropy and Clark, Crawford, Swink

### Takeaway
Anthropy treats verbs as the most important rules and gets their variety from authored **scenes**, the content-driven route. Clark recasts play as a **dialogue**. Crawford requires a partner that **thinks**, and calls responding to a non-thinking world "reactivity". Swink's game feel is about how a physical verb feels to control (real-time control, polish), not about depth.

### Cited Findings
- *A Game Design Vocabulary* (2014) has two parts. Part I, by Anthropy, covers Language, Verbs and Objects, Scenes, and Context. Part II, by Clark, covers Creating Dialogue and Resistance. — [source] [Pearson sample pages](https://ptgmedia.pearsoncmg.com/images/9780321886927/samplepages/0321886925.pdf)
- Summaries say verbs are framed as the most important rules because they let the player change the game state. Good design puts verbs where they create interesting choices and interact meaningfully with other verbs. — [source] [First Person Scholar review](https://www.firstpersonscholar.com/a-game-design-vocabulary/) (snippet, not read in full)
- Clark's part treats the game as a conversation. Resources act as rewards that switch verbs on, e.g. ammo enables "shoot". — [source] [First Person Scholar review](https://www.firstpersonscholar.com/a-game-design-vocabulary/)
- Crawford defines interactivity as "a cyclic process between two or more active agents in which each agent alternately listens, thinks, and speaks". — [source] [O'Reilly, *Chris Crawford on Game Design*, ch. 2](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/chris-crawford-on/9780133119671/ch02.html); [ludism.org summary](https://ludism.org/gamedesign/Interactivity)
- Crawford: dodging a falling tree limb "is not interactivity, it's reactivity". — [source] [ludism.org](https://ludism.org/gamedesign/Interactivity)
- Crawford: verbs lie at the heart of what we do with computers, and listing every verb a player can perform reveals what a game is about. — [source] [Crawford, iDMAa journal article](https://idmaa.org/journalarticle-v1n3-the-two-faces-of-reality/)
- Swink defines game feel as "real-time control of virtual objects in a simulated space, with interactions emphasised by polish". He lists experiences such as the aesthetic sensation of control and the pleasure of mastering a skill. Critics note the definition leaves out games without real-time control. — [source] [Wikipedia: Game feel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_feel)

### Inferences
- [opinion] Anthropy's scene-based approach is the content route: the same few verbs stay interesting because the designer authors new scenes that re-contextualise them. That is excellent for a single-player game and expensive for a low-content one. Clark's dialogue framing and Crawford's "agent that thinks" point the other way: the cheapest second agent that truly thinks is another player.
- [opinion] Crawford's test separates the user's verbs into two kinds. A verb aimed at the world (jump, push a crate) is reactive: depth must come from authored levels or physics emergence. A verb aimed at, or read by, another person (signal, hand over, block, lie) is interactive in his sense.
- [opinion] Swink's feel makes a physical verb pleasant per press, but feel is not depth. A friendslop game with great-feeling verbs and no player coupling still runs out of fun once the levels run out. Feel buys the first minutes; coupling buys the hundredth session.

### Gaps
- I did not read Anthropy's chapter text, so specific claims (e.g. "few verbs, many contexts") are unverified.
- Crawford's interactivity definition is quoted via secondary pages. The primary book text (*The Art of Interactive Design*, 2002) was not read.
- Swink's exact wording came via Wikipedia, not the book.

## 5. Chris Hecker on SpyParty: human behaviour as the game

### Takeaway
Hecker frames SpyParty as an inverse Turing test. The spy, a human, must act like the AI party guests while the sniper reads behaviour. The depth is in human perception and deception, built on "known and compelling interactions" made mechanically deep, not on authored content volume.

### Cited Findings
- "When you force a human to act like a computer, you get a game – it's an interesting task." — [read] [Game Developer, Hecker at UC Berkeley](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/hecker-visits-uc-berkeley-to-talk-i-spyparty-i-future-of-emotion-in-games)
- "I think it's better to start with known and compelling interactions and find a way to make them mechanically deep." — [read] [same article](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/hecker-visits-uc-berkeley-to-talk-i-spyparty-i-future-of-emotion-in-games)
- On other games: "Very rarely do you interact with someone in any meaningful way." Also: "I think this sort of subtlety is part of where games need to go." — [read] [same article](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/hecker-visits-uc-berkeley-to-talk-i-spyparty-i-future-of-emotion-in-games)
- The article describes the spy–sniper relationship as uneven (asymmetric) and hinging on the threat of violence rather than violence. — [read] [same article](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/hecker-visits-uc-berkeley-to-talk-i-spyparty-i-future-of-emotion-in-games)
- On plans and goals: "There's going to be multiple spies at the same party, sometime co-operative, sometimes competitive." "An approach is to build up levels of interaction that are meaningful to people who are sick of killing orcs." — [read] [MCV, Inside Chris Hecker's SpyParty](https://mcvuk.com/development-news/inside-chris-heckeras-spyparty/)
- The game is self-described as a competitive espionage game about human behaviour, performance, perception and deception. — [source] [Steam page](https://store.steampowered.com/app/329070) (snippet; spyparty.com/about was down)
- Hecker reportedly prefers "depth first, accessibility later" playtesting, crediting a Rob Pardo (Blizzard) talk. — [source] [Game Informer interview, 2011](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2011/06/08/chris-hecker-spy-party-interview.aspx) (snippet, not read)

### Inferences
- [opinion] SpyParty is the clearest case of "low content, high coupling between players". There is one room per match and a small set of mission actions, and the depth lives entirely in two minds reading each other. That is Björk's asymmetric information plus indirect information, done with body language as the channel.
- [opinion] The AI guests are content. Their job is only to be a believable baseline the human must imitate. That is a cheap kind of content: a background to hide against, not a challenge to consume.
- [opinion] Lesson for co-op: Hecker's idea of several spies at one party, sometimes co-operative and sometimes competitive, points to semi-co-op hidden roles as a way to keep human reading inside a co-op frame.

### Gaps
- I could not reach spyparty.com/about or Hecker's GDC talk pages. The phrase "the game is about human perception" is not verified as his wording.

## 6. Raph Koster, *A Theory of Fun*: patterns get learned, then boring

### Takeaway
Koster's fun is learning a pattern. Once the pattern is learned ("chunked"), the activity bores. New enemies that need the same tactic add no new challenge. Koster says outright that the puzzle "sometimes" comes "from a computer, sometimes from another player". Content solves boredom one consumed unit at a time; other players keep producing new puzzles.

### Cited Findings
- "fun is the feeling you get when you are exercising your brain by solving a cognitive puzzle." — [read] [Koster, An atomic theory of fun game design (2012)](https://www.raphkoster.com/2012/01/24/an-atomic-theory-of-fun-game-design/)
- "Sometimes the puzzle is provided by a computer, sometimes by another player." — [read] [same essay](https://www.raphkoster.com/2012/01/24/an-atomic-theory-of-fun-game-design/)
- A core mechanic "is probably interesting only for a limited amount of time". On adding enemies, "you have not actually added a new challenge to the game", and "This is where players find repetition". He also says "each enemy provides a unique puzzle", in a different context. — [read] [same essay](https://www.raphkoster.com/2012/01/24/an-atomic-theory-of-fun-game-design/) (fragments; check the surrounding text before quoting the enemy line)
- Checkers: "This variety is what makes checkers an interesting game." He also discusses team encounters designed so that "it takes teamwork to resolve them", and notes that crafting systems "do not require interdependence between players at the actual crafting step". — [read] [same essay](https://www.raphkoster.com/2012/01/24/an-atomic-theory-of-fun-game-design/)
- "Games that fail to exercise the brain become boring." (*A Theory of Fun*, p. 38, as quoted in a raphkoster.com comment thread.) — [source, weak] search snippet; the exact page among the raphkoster.com hits was not identified (candidates: [Brenda's games](https://www.raphkoster.com/2009/04/30/brendas-games/), [Worldcon panel](https://www.raphkoster.com/2006/08/26/worldcon-world-of-warcrack-panel/))
- Koster: if something is not novel we already have a chunk for it, and even the same rollercoaster gets boring once absorbed and chunked. — [source, weak] search snippet from Koster's blog; exact post not identified ([raphkoster.com author archive hit](https://www.raphkoster.com/author/raph/page/500/))
- A reviewer reads Koster as saying that human opponents give an endless supply of puzzles (chess). That is the reviewer's reading, not a Koster quote. — [source] [Habrador blog](https://blog.habrador.com/2016/10/why-are-games-boring-or-fun.html)

### Inferences
- [opinion] In Koster's terms, content cost is the price of new patterns. A content-led game buys each new pattern by hand (enemy, level), and once that pattern is chunked the money is spent. Another player is a pattern source that changes as the other player learns, so it does not chunk as fast. This holds as long as the verbs let players' choices matter to each other, i.e. not in a race / multiplayer-solitaire setup.
- [opinion] Koster's crafting remark (no interdependence at the crafting step) is a direct example of a loop with zero player coupling, the thing the user's formula avoids.

### Gaps
- I did not read the book itself. The famous line "the destiny of games is to become boring" comes from memory only and is unverified.

## 7. Typologies of multiplayer interaction: Manninen, Zagal/Rick/Hsi, Stenros et al.

### Takeaway
Manninen lists interaction *forms* (gesture, body language, avatar look, control actions and so on) and finds that multiplayer games use a limited set of them. Zagal, Rick and Hsi give the most usable co-op rules. Build tension between what is best for me and what is best for the team. Let players act without team consent. Make payoffs traceable to choices. Give different abilities. Otherwise you get one player running the team, and replay needs ever-changing challenges, which means content. Stenros et al. separate sociability *around* a game from social play *mediated by* its rules.

### Cited Findings
- Manninen's taxonomy sorts interactions by purpose, context and acting entities. It was built from video recordings, interviews, walkthroughs and observation across about twenty games. Its categories include Avatar Appearance, Autonomous & Automatic Actions, Gestures, Body Language and control actions. — [source] [Manninen, Game Studies 3(1), 2003](https://gamestudies.org/0301/manninen/); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/220200727_Interaction_Forms_and_Communicative_Actions_in_Multiplayer_Games) (snippets; gamestudies.org refused the connection)
- Manninen uses Habermas's communicative action theory. He finds the communicative side of multiplayer games is enabled by "a relatively limited set of interaction forms". — [source] [Game Studies 0301](https://gamestudies.org/0301/manninen/)
- Zagal, Rick and Hsi, "Collaborative games: Lessons learned from board games", *Simulation & Gaming* 37(1), 2006, pp. 24–40. Their case study is Knizia's *Lord of the Rings* board game. — [source] [EconPapers record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:sae:simgam:v:37:y:2006:i:1:p:24-40); [paper PDF (403 on fetch)](https://www.eng.utah.edu/~zagal/Papers/Zagal%20et%20al%20-%20Collaborative%20Games%20-%20Lessons%20learned%20from%20boardgames.pdf)
- Definitions as reported:
  - Competitive: players "pitted against" each other.
  - Cooperative: some working together, but rewards are not shared equally.
  - Collaborative: games that "only reward collaboration, and all players gain or suffer equally".

  — [read] [grdphil review of the paper](https://grdphil.blogspot.com/2010/11/review-collaborative-games-lessons.html) (secondary)
- Four lessons, as reported:
  1. "introduce a tension between perceived individual utility and team utility";
  2. players should be able to act "without the consent of the team";
  3. players must be able to trace payoffs back to their own decisions;
  4. give players "different abilities or responsibilities".

  — [read] [grdphil review](https://grdphil.blogspot.com/2010/11/review-collaborative-games-lessons.html)
- Three pitfalls, as reported:
  1. without enough reason to collaborate, the game slides into "one player making the decisions for the team";
  2. players must care about, and be satisfied by, the outcome;
  3. replay needs "the experience needs to be different each time", with evolving challenges.

  — [read] [grdphil review](https://grdphil.blogspot.com/2010/11/review-collaborative-games-lessons.html)
- The review also reports the paper's point that online play loses identity and personality cues and carries more risk of deception, but gives control over which kinds of communication are allowed. — [read] [grdphil review](https://grdphil.blogspot.com/2010/11/review-collaborative-games-lessons.html)
- Stenros, Paavilainen and Mäyrä distinguish the sociability players engage in *around* the game from the social play *contained and mediated by* the game ("Social interaction in games", IJART 2011). Their earlier paper is "The many faces of sociability and social play in games", MindTrek 2009, pp. 82–89. — [source] [Inderscience abstract](https://inderscience.com/offers.php?id=41486); [OpenAlex record](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1145%2F1621841.1621857)

### Inferences
- [opinion] Fully collaborative co-op loses the cheapest variety source, an opposing mind, and Zagal pitfall 3 then pushes it back onto content ("evolving challenges"). The fixes that stay cheap are Zagal lesson 1 (personal vs team tension), lesson 4 (different abilities, so players need each other) and Björk's asymmetric information (so they must talk). All three make players the source of new situations instead of levels.
- [opinion] Zagal pitfall 1, the "alpha player", is the failure that hits low-content co-op hardest. If the puzzle is fully visible and solvable, one player solves it and the rest follow orders. Hidden or split information and real-time simultaneous action (where no one can direct everyone) block this.
- [opinion] Stenros' split is a caution for friendslop. Much of the fun in these games may be sociability around the game (voice-chat banter, laughing at physics). That is free, but it is not depth produced by the rules. A game that only hosts sociability depends on the friend group bringing the fun. Social play mediated by the game (verbs that couple players) is the part the designer actually builds.
- [opinion] Manninen's "limited set of interaction forms" suggests an open space: verbs that use underused channels (gesture, body language, avatar look, as in SpyParty) can couple players without new content.

- I could not read Manninen's full category list and definitions (connection refused). The summary list above is partial.
- I did not read the Stenros et al. 2009 full text. The 2011 distinction comes from its abstract only. A related Mäyrä et al. PDF ([homepages.tuni.fi](https://homepages.tuni.fi/frans.mayra/Mayra-et_al-Multiplayer2.pdf)) was found but not read.

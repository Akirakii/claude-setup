# Cross-case analysis of many postmortems / qualitative documents to find repeated failure patterns

Verification tags used below: **[read]** = I read the primary text (PDF full text or HTML full text); **[abstract/snippet]** = only abstract, search summary or secondary page seen; **[unverified]** = from background knowledge, no source fetched this session; treat as a lead, not a fact.

## Q1. What are the exact steps of each method, and what goes in and comes out of each?

### Takeaway
Every method found has the same skeleton: (1) pick and filter documents, (2) cut each document into units (one problem / one "what went right/wrong" item / one quote), (3) assign each unit a code from a list that starts either from prior literature or from nothing and grows until no new codes appear (saturation), (4) check coders agree, (5) count units per category across documents and compare subgroups. The game-postmortem studies (Washburn 2016, Politowski 2020/2021) are "seeded list + grow it + count" studies; reflexive TA and grounded theory aim at interpretive themes/theory rather than counts.

### Cited Findings

#### A. Washburn et al. 2016 (ICSE SEIP), 155 Gamasutra postmortems [read, full PDF text]
- Step 0 – Corpus. Input: 215 postmortems listed on Gamasutra.com before Jan 2014. Output: 155 usable postmortems; 60 ignored as off-topic (e.g. a GDC experience report, an article on one tool, or one aspect of process instead of the whole project). Inclusion rule: "If a case was in fact a postmortem review for a game, and detailed what went right and what went wrong during development, then it was used." — [Washburn et al. 2016 PDF](https://thomas-zimmermann.com/publications/files/washburn-icse-2016.pdf)
- Coders: two authors (Michael Washburn, Pavithra Sathiyanarayanan) coded every postmortem. Unit coded = each item in the "what went right" and "what went wrong" sections; contextual table at the end of each postmortem was used for metadata (platform, team size, dev time, publisher). — [Washburn et al. 2016 PDF](https://thomas-zimmermann.com/publications/files/washburn-icse-2016.pdf)
- Step 1 – Seed list. Input: Ara Shirinian's earlier analysis of 24 postmortems (2008–2010). Output: 12 predetermined categories. — [Washburn et al. 2016 PDF](https://thomas-zimmermann.com/publications/files/washburn-icse-2016.pdf)
- Step 2 – Iterative category building, 3 weekly iterations. Week 1: each coder classified 3 postmortems into the 12 categories, noted new categories, and *revisited already-coded reviews to update them*. Week 2: 10 postmortems each. Week 3: 15 each. After each iteration the two discussed new categories "and determined if they were viable." Output after 60 postmortems: a frozen list of 22 categories (stopped adding new ones). — [Washburn et al. 2016 PDF](https://thomas-zimmermann.com/publications/files/washburn-icse-2016.pdf)
- Step 3 – Bulk coding. Input: frozen 22 categories + remaining ~95 postmortems. Rate ~40 postmortems/week combined; weekly review "to ensure we both had the same understanding of each category." No kappa or agreement statistic reported; threat-to-validity mitigation was "having two authors discuss their qualitative tagging frequently." — [Washburn et al. 2016 PDF](https://thomas-zimmermann.com/publications/files/washburn-icse-2016.pdf)
- Final category list (22 sub-categories in 5 groups), extracted from the paper's Section 5: **Product** (Art, Creativity, Features, Game Design, Gameplay, Product Evolution, Scope); **Development** (Development Process, Documents, Obstacles, Team, Testing, Tools); **Resources** (Budget, Hardware, Publisher Relations, Schedule); **Customer facing** (Community Support, Feedback, Marketing, Piracy/Licensing); **Other** (e.g. luck, self-funding, risks paying off). Every category can appear as both a best practice and a pitfall. — [Washburn et al. 2016 PDF](https://thomas-zimmermann.com/publications/files/washburn-icse-2016.pdf)
- Step 4 – Counting. Output: % of postmortems that mention each category as right (Fig. 2) and as wrong (Fig. 3); percentages don't sum to 100 because a postmortem lists several categories. Top "went right": game design 50%, team 40%, art 39%, development process. Top "went wrong": obstacles 37%, schedule 25%, development process 24%, game design 22%. — [Washburn et al. 2016 PDF](https://thomas-zimmermann.com/publications/files/washburn-icse-2016.pdf)
- Step 5 – Subgroup comparison by metadata (Section 7): team ≤20 vs >20, publisher vs self-published, single vs multi-platform. Examples: 50% of small teams vs 26% of large teams listed obstacles as wrong; 30% of self-published vs 19% with publisher listed testing as right. Comparisons are raw percentage differences, no significance tests reported. — [Washburn et al. 2016 PDF](https://thomas-zimmermann.com/publications/files/washburn-icse-2016.pdf)
- Step 6 – Report. Per category: description, a quoted example of right and of wrong, and a "Takeaway" recommendation (e.g. "To avoid schedule slippage, game developers need to spend more time to plan out all the work…"). Raw postmortems and coded data put in an online appendix. — [Washburn et al. 2016 PDF](https://thomas-zimmermann.com/publications/files/washburn-icse-2016.pdf)

#### B. Politowski et al. 2020, "Dataset of Video Game Development Problems" (MSR 2020 data showcase) [read, full PDF text]
- Process (their Fig. 1, a loop): random-pick 20 postmortems per author from Gamasutra (1998–2018) → each author reads the "What went wrong" section using "a coding technique from Grounded Theory" (citing Stol, Ralph & Fitzgerald 2016) → identifies all problems, extracts quotes, groups similar problems against a **catalog seeded from prior literature** (Petrillo et al.; Washburn et al.) → new problem types added to catalog with a description → group meeting: "discuss the findings, review some doubts, and analyse the correctness of the catalog"; any change updates both catalog and problem database, "which sometimes lead us to re-read the postmortems" → repeat "until no more new type of problems appears" (saturation). — [Politowski et al. 2020, arXiv 2001.00491](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.00491)
- Selection deviations: some postmortems picked by hand to balance years; postmortems with no useful development-process information were discarded. — [Politowski et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.00491)
- Output: 200 games, 1,035 problems, 20 types, 3 groups (Production, Management, Business); median 5 problems per game. — [Politowski et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.00491); [MSR 2020 listing](https://2020.msrconf.org/details/msr-2020-Data-showcase/3/Dataset-of-Video-Game-Development-Problems)
- The 20 types with the catalog's one-line descriptions (Table 1): Design ("any problem regarding the design of the game, like balancing the gameplay. Not a technical detail"); Documentation; Tools (engines, APIs, dev kits, third-party software); Technical (team code/asset infrastructure); Testing; Bugs; Prototyping ("lack of or no prototyping phase nor validation of the gameplay/feature"); Unrealistic Scope; Feature Creep ("adding unplanned new features … during its implementation"); Cutting Features; Delays; Crunch Time; Communication; Team (setup, loss of staff, outsourcing); Over Budget; Multiple Projects; Planning; Security (leaked assets); Marketing; Monetization. — [Politowski et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.00491)
- Storage: first JSON validated by JSON Schema, then migrated to CSV, one line per problem, for R scripts. Hosted at GitHub `game-dev-database/postmortem-problems`; contributions via pull request, curated. — [Politowski et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.00491); [Politowski et al. 2021, arXiv 2009.02440 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2009.02440)

#### C. Politowski et al. 2021, "Game Industry Problems: an Extensive Analysis of the Gray Literature" (arXiv 2009.02440; IST) [read via HTML full text summary]
- Corpus: 200 Gamasutra postmortems 1997–2019 → 927 problems in 20 types (note: differs from the 2020 dataset paper's 1,035 problems / 1998–2018; the papers don't reconcile this, likely a cleaned/refactored version — **inference, unverified**). — [arXiv 2009.02440](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.02440)
- Same coding loop as the dataset paper; seed list from Petrillo et al. and Washburn et al.; types added on consensus; **type list stabilised at about 140 reviews**. — [arXiv 2009.02440 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2009.02440)
- Groups here are four: Production, People Management, Feature Management, Business; split 46% production, 45% management, 9% business. — [arXiv 2009.02440 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2009.02440)
- "Root-cause analysis" step = a second pass: authors re-read all problems and assigned 105 finer sub-types; reported top-10 with counts, e.g. Insufficient workforce (49), Environmental problems (48), Wrong marketing strategy (35); each sub-type gets an explanation plus developers' own proposed solutions quoted from the postmortems. No formal RCA technique (no 5-whys/fishbone) is described; conclusion "majority of the main root causes are related to people, not technologies" rests on this sub-type pass. — [arXiv 2009.02440 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2009.02440); [abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.02440)
- Trend analysis: counts normalised per year and fitted with a second-degree polynomial with 0.95 CI; the paper describes normalisation as by problems/year in one place and by postmortems/year in the threats section (unreconciled). No inter-rater statistic reported. — [arXiv 2009.02440 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2009.02440)

#### D. Other cross-project failure studies
- **FAIL (Anandayuvaraj, Campbell, Tewari, Davis; ASE 2024)**: LLM pipeline over news. Steps: collect news articles → classify relevance → merge articles describing the same incident → analyse each incident against existing postmortem / fault / system-characteristic taxonomies. Tuned on 31 manually analysed failures; evaluation F1 90% for collecting relevant news, V-measure 0.98 for merging, ~90% of failure facts extracted. Applied to 137,427 articles (11 outlets, 2010–2022) → 2,457 distinct failures from 4,184 articles; reports high recurrence of similar failures within and across organisations. ~US$50 per year of data to update. [abstract/snippet] — [FAIL arXiv 2406.08221](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08221v2); [ASE 2024 listing](https://conf.researchr.org/details/ase-2024/ase-2024-research/41/FAIL-Analyzing-Software-Failures-from-the-News-Using-LLMs)
- Follow-up uses FAIL data to compare failures across industries with LLMs. [abstract/snippet] — [arXiv 2408.03528](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.03528v2)
- Interview-based failure-learning study (space research centre, 10 interviews): two analysts, Braun & Clarke TA; 5 transcripts co-coded to build a codebook of 58 code categories from 343 memos; one transcript independently coded at 92% percentage agreement; saturation after the 8th interview. — [arXiv 2509.06301](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.06301v2)
- **NASA LLIS**: each lesson contains a summary of the original "driving event" and recommendations, reviewed before publication; KSC form KDP-KSC-P-2393 has sections Abstract, Driving Event, Lesson(s) Learned, and reviewer checks (does the driving event say what happened, where and when; are recommendations clear suggestions drawn from the event). [snippet] — [NASA lessons learned page](https://www.nasa.gov/?p=262828); [KSC form](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/kdp-ksc-p-2393-revd-scof.pdf?emrc=6ad8b360ba94f); [data.gov dataset](https://catalog-beta.data.gov/dataset/nasa-engineering-network-lessons-learned)
- **Aviation ASRS**: reports are coded by analysts with a fixed hierarchical taxonomy: Anomaly (top-level, e.g. Deviation–Procedural, Ground Excursion → subtypes like Runway/Taxiway/Clearance, each with numeric code), Primary Problem, Contributing Factors/Situations, Detector, Human Factors (added since 2009). Reportedly analysts assign one primary and up to four contributing factors from a list of sixteen [snippet, not verified in form itself]. — [ASRS Coding Taxonomy PDF](https://asrs.arc.nasa.gov/docs/dbol/ASRS_CodingTaxonomy.pdf); [FAA ASRS data dictionary](https://asip.faa.gov/explore/asrs/info/glossary)

#### E. Qualitative methods (generic)
- **Reflexive TA, Braun & Clarke, current six phases (2021 wording)**: "1) data familiarisation and writing familiarisation notes; 2) systematic data coding; 3) generating initial themes from coded and collated data; 4) developing and reviewing themes; 5) refining, defining and naming themes; and 6) writing the report." Phases are not rigid and become recursive with skill. Phase 3 was relabelled from "searching for themes" to "generating initial themes" to stress the researcher creates themes ("always avoid claiming that themes emerged"). [read] — [Braun & Clarke 2021, "One size fits all?"](https://www.sfu.ca/~palys/Braun&Clarke-2021-ReflexiveThematicAnalysis.pdf)
  - I/O per phase (my mapping, **inference**): 1 in raw docs → out familiarisation notes; 2 in docs → out coded extracts (code label + text span); 3 in collated codes → out candidate themes (clusters of codes around a central idea); 4 in candidate themes + full dataset → out revised theme map; 5 in theme map → out theme definitions and names; 6 in definitions + extracts → out report.
  - A theme = "pattern of shared meaning, cohering around a central concept"; a one-word bucket like "format" or "disliked" is a *topic summary*, better called a code; "For themes to be patterns of shared meaning … they must be analytic outputs, not inputs." [read] — [Braun & Clarke 2021](https://www.sfu.ca/~palys/Braun&Clarke-2021-ReflexiveThematicAnalysis.pdf)
- **Three TA schools** (Braun & Clarke): *coding reliability* TA (fixed codebook, multiple coders, inter-rater reliability as quality measure, themes often set before analysis); *codebook* TA (framework analysis, template analysis: structured coding frame, themes mostly set early but refinable, IRR usually not a quality measure); *reflexive* TA ("a research team is not required or even desirable"). [read] — [Braun & Clarke 2021](https://www.sfu.ca/~palys/Braun&Clarke-2021-ReflexiveThematicAnalysis.pdf)
- **Grounded theory variants**: Glaser (classic): open coding → theoretical coding, categories via constant comparison; Strauss & Corbin: open → axial (relate categories with the conditions–actions/interactions–consequences "paradigm model") → selective (integrate around a core category); Charmaz (constructivist): initial coding (close to data, often line-by-line) → focused coding (most frequent/significant codes applied to larger data) → theoretical coding, with memo-writing to raise codes to categories. Memos in all variants. [secondary guides] — [Which grounded theory?](https://www.groundedtheoryonline.com/which-grounded-theory/); [Charmaz 2006 chapter PDF](https://is.muni.cz/el/fss/jaro2016/SOC560/um/61907340/Charmaz_2006.pdf?lang=cs); [Bryant & Charmaz chapter](https://is.muni.cz/el/fss/jaro2013/MVZ453/um/SAGE_bryant_charmaz_191-214.pdf?kod=C2184)
- In SE, Stol, Ralph & Fitzgerald (ICSE 2016) reviewed 98 GT-mentioning SE articles: 52 claimed GT, 46 used only GT techniques, only 16 described procedures in detail; warn of "method slurring" (using a subset of GT practices and calling it GT). The game-postmortem studies "use a coding technique from GT" rather than full GT (theoretical sampling, a core category) — the inference that they are GT-technique studies is mine. — [Stol et al. 2016](https://cora.ucc.ie/handle/10468/7041)
- **Card sorting**: open sort (sorters create and name groups), closed sort (sort into predefined categories, used later to validate labels), hybrid. Zimmermann's chapter "Card-sorting: From text to themes" applies it to open-ended text responses (steps not fetched). — [O'Reilly, Perspectives on Data Science for SE](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/perspectives-on-data/9780128042618/B9780128042069000271.xhtml); [User Interviews field guide](https://userinterviews.com/ux-research-field-guide-chapter/card-sorting); [GOV.UK user research blog](https://userresearch.blog.gov.uk/2018/03/23/how-we-refined-our-approach-to-card-sorting/)
- **Affinity diagram / KJ method** (Jiro Kawakita, 1960s): one idea per card → silently group related cards until all placed → stack near-duplicates, cluster small groups into larger → write a header card per group (loner cards may stand alone) → draw arrows for relations; output can feed a fishbone diagram. Gives no priority ordering by itself. [secondary] — [Wikipedia: Affinity diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affinity_diagram); [project-management.com KJ](https://project-management.com/affinity-diagram-kawakita-jiro-or-kj-method/)
- **Template analysis (Nigel King)**: start with a few *a priori* themes, code a subset of documents, build an initial hierarchical template, apply it to further documents and modify (insert/delete/re-level themes) until stable, then use final template to interpret. **[unverified — no source fetched]**; Braun & Clarke class it as codebook TA. — [Braun & Clarke 2021](https://www.sfu.ca/~palys/Braun&Clarke-2021-ReflexiveThematicAnalysis.pdf)

### Inferences
- For "many postmortems → repeated failure patterns", the Washburn/Politowski design (seeded codebook + growth to saturation + per-document counts + metadata splits) is the direct precedent; reflexive TA fits better when the goal is *why* patterns happen, not *how often*.
- Politowski's 105 sub-types are effectively Charmaz-style focused codes under 20 topic-level types; their "root causes" are a finer coding pass, not causal analysis.

## Q2. When do you start from a known category list vs build categories from the data?

### Takeaway
All three game-postmortem studies did both: seed from earlier literature (Shirinian's categories → 12; Petrillo/Washburn → catalog), let new categories in during a pilot, then freeze the list once new categories stop appearing (60 postmortems for Washburn; ~140 for Politowski). Pure a-priori lists (ASRS, closed sort, coding-reliability TA) fit when categories must be comparable across years/coders; pure bottom-up (open coding, open sort, KJ, reflexive TA) fits when the domain is new.

### Cited Findings
- Washburn: 12 seed categories from Shirinian → 22 after 3 iterations / 60 postmortems, then frozen. — [Washburn et al. 2016](https://thomas-zimmermann.com/publications/files/washburn-icse-2016.pdf)
- Politowski: "Previous definitions were used to create a catalog of problems … We add each new problem type absent from the catalog with its description"; stop when no new type appears. — [Politowski et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.00491); stabilised ~140 reviews — [Politowski 2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2009.02440)
- Closed card sorts were not useful early in taxonomy creation because there were no good categories yet; open first, closed to validate. — [GOV.UK blog](https://userresearch.blog.gov.uk/2018/03/23/how-we-refined-our-approach-to-card-sorting/); [User Interviews](https://userinterviews.com/ux-research-field-guide-chapter/card-sorting)
- Coding-reliability and codebook TA develop themes early or before analysis; Braun & Clarke warn themes fixed before coding "are unlikely … much more than topic summaries", and that a study with a pre-defined purpose (e.g. sort into helpful/unhelpful) suits codebook/coding-reliability TA better than reflexive TA. — [Braun & Clarke 2021](https://www.sfu.ca/~palys/Braun&Clarke-2021-ReflexiveThematicAnalysis.pdf)
- ASRS and FAIL use fixed external taxonomies so data from many reports/years is comparable. — [ASRS taxonomy](https://asrs.arc.nasa.gov/docs/dbol/ASRS_CodingTaxonomy.pdf); [FAIL](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08221v2)
- Strauss & Corbin's paradigm model is criticised for pushing data into predefined relations. — [groundedtheoryonline](https://www.groundedtheoryonline.com/which-grounded-theory/)

### Inferences
- Practical rule distilled from the precedents: seed from an existing list (e.g. Politowski's 20 types) → pilot ~10–20% of documents with permission to add codes and recode earlier ones → freeze → code the rest; keep an "Other" bucket and review it at the end (Washburn kept "Other").
- Seeding risks topic-summary categories ("Design", "Team") that count well but explain little; a second, finer pass (Politowski's sub-types) adds the explanatory layer.

### Gaps
- No source found giving a quantitative rule for when to freeze a codebook beyond "no new codes".

## Q3. How do you count and compare patterns across projects, and how many cases are needed?

### Takeaway
Count at document level ("% of postmortems that mention category X", so one verbose postmortem can't dominate) or item level ("% of all problems"), then split by metadata (team size, publisher, platform, year). Case counts in precedents: 155 (Washburn), 200 (Politowski); category lists saturated at 60 and ~140 documents respectively; interview studies saturate far earlier (8th interview in one study).

### Cited Findings
- Washburn unit = postmortem: "percentage of postmortem reviews which list each category"; subgroup percentages compared directly (no tests). — [Washburn et al. 2016](https://thomas-zimmermann.com/publications/files/washburn-icse-2016.pdf)
- Politowski unit = problem (927 or 1,035), with group shares (46/45/9%), sub-type counts (e.g. 49 insufficient workforce), and per-year normalised trends fitted with a 2nd-degree polynomial with 95% CI. — [Politowski 2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2009.02440)
- Median 5 problems per game. — [Politowski 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.00491)
- FAIL found recurrence by matching incidents to taxonomy categories over 2,457 failures. — [FAIL](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08221v2)
- Saturation after 8 of 10 interviews (median 33 codes/interview). — [arXiv 2509.06301](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.06301v2)
- Braun & Clarke question data saturation as a concept for reflexive TA (Braun & Clarke 2019c "To saturate or not to saturate?"). — [Braun & Clarke 2021 references](https://www.sfu.ca/~palys/Braun&Clarke-2021-ReflexiveThematicAnalysis.pdf)
- Guest, Bunce & Johnson (2006, Field Methods) is often cited for saturation around 12 interviews — **[unverified; only seen as a reference title in Braun & Clarke 2021]**.

### Inferences
- Document-level presence/absence is the safer count for self-reported postmortems, since length varies; item-level counts are needed for sub-type ranking.
- Postmortems are self-reported and selectively candid (Washburn's threat 3), so counts measure "what authors chose to write", not true failure frequency.
- With ~5 problems per document and 20 types, tens of documents surface the common types, but subgroup comparisons (e.g. small vs large teams) need enough per cell; precedents gave no significance tests, so differences of a few points should not be read as real.

### Gaps
- No source found giving a power/sample-size rule for comparing category frequencies across subgroups of postmortems.

## Q4. How is a single tagged item structured (fields)?

### Takeaway
One row = one problem quote, linked to its source document and to project metadata, plus the code (group/type/sub-type). Incident systems add causal slots (primary problem, contributing factors, detector) and a recommendation.

### Cited Findings
- Politowski dataset record (CSV, one line per problem): ID, Title, Year, Source (URL), Name (game), Platform (1–3 of PC/Console/Mobile), Genre (1–12 of Action…Running), Mode (1–3 of Single/Multi/Online), Group, Type, SubType, Quote (verbatim, unmodified). Data model: Postmortem (title, year, source) 1–1 Game (name) 1–* Problem (quote) → ProblemType → ProblemGroup. — [Politowski 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.00491); [Politowski 2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2009.02440)
- Example row: Title "Baldurs Gate II – The Anatomy of a Sequel", Year 2001, Source http://bit.ly/2IDsVa0, Name Baldur's Gate II, Group Production, Type Testing, SubType "Scope too big to test properly", Quote about pairing testers with designers to check each quest. — [Politowski 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.00491); [Politowski 2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2009.02440)
- Other coded examples: P#604 insufficient workforce (one person did design, PM and external duties for six months); P#430 wrong marketing strategy (~100 streamers emailed, zero streams in first wave); P#417 underestimation (5 people/6 months plan → 8 people/24 months, >700% off). — [Politowski 2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2009.02440)
- Washburn coded item (implied structure): postmortem, section (right/wrong), category, quote; plus per-postmortem metadata platform, team size, dev length, publisher. Example: Black & White, wrong, Schedule, Molyneux: "I have a reputation for being, shall we say, optimistic…". — [Washburn et al. 2016](https://thomas-zimmermann.com/publications/files/washburn-icse-2016.pdf)
- ASRS record fields: Anomaly (hierarchical codes), Primary Problem, Contributing Factors/Situations, Detector, Human Factors, plus narrative. — [FAA ASRS data dictionary](https://asip.faa.gov/explore/asrs/info/glossary); [ASRS taxonomy](https://asrs.arc.nasa.gov/docs/dbol/ASRS_CodingTaxonomy.pdf)
- NASA LLIS record: Abstract, Driving Event (what, where, when), Lesson(s) Learned, Recommendation(s), organisation, date. [snippet] — [KSC form](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/kdp-ksc-p-2393-revd-scof.pdf?emrc=6ad8b360ba94f); [NASA page](https://www.nasa.gov/?p=262828)
- Campbell et al. 2013 code *primary and secondary* codes per text unit and standardise units first ("unitization"). [abstract] — [Campbell et al. 2013](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0049124113500475)

### Inferences
- A merged item template drawing on these: id, source doc + locator, verbatim quote, polarity (went right/wrong), group, type, sub-type, cause/contributing factor, consequence, author's proposed fix, coder, date coded, coder memo, confidence. The last four are not in Politowski's schema; they come from audit-trail practice (see Q5).

## Q5. Reliability: inter-rater agreement, negotiated agreement, and what a single coder can do

### Takeaway
Neither game-postmortem study reports kappa; both used discussion to reach consensus. In reflexive TA, inter-rater reliability is considered unnecessary or incoherent. In codebook/coding-reliability work, agreement is measured (Cohen's kappa) or reached by negotiation. A single coder can use an audit trail, re-code after a gap (intra-rater check), or use an LLM as a second deductive coder. LLM agreement with humans is uneven: kappa roughly 0.38–0.79 depending on code and prompt, usually below human-human agreement.

### Cited Findings
- Washburn: two coders, weekly alignment discussion, no statistic. — [Washburn et al. 2016](https://thomas-zimmermann.com/publications/files/washburn-icse-2016.pdf)
- Politowski: consensus discussion per iteration, no inter-rater statistic, acknowledges interpretation may vary by researcher. — [Politowski 2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2009.02440)
- Braun & Clarke: coding-reliability TA uses a fixed codebook, independent coders, inter-rater reliability and consensus; for reflexive TA, applying coding-reliability to avoid "bias" is "illogical, incoherent". — [Braun & Clarke 2021](https://www.sfu.ca/~palys/Braun&Clarke-2021-ReflexiveThematicAnalysis.pdf)
- Negotiated agreement (Campbell et al. 2013): standardise the text units, then refine the scheme until agreement is acceptable. After negotiation, agreement reached 96% (primary + secondary codes) and 97% (primary codes). The approach is meant for the case where "a single knowledgeable coder will code all the transcripts once the coding scheme has been established" (so: two coders calibrate the scheme, then one coder codes everything). They cite Garrison et al. 2006 on negotiated coding. [abstract/summary] — [Campbell et al. 2013, SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0049124113500475); [CBS record](https://research.cbs.dk/en/publications/coding-in-depth-semistructured-interviews-problems-of-unitization/)
- Percentage agreement used as the measure: 92% on one independently coded transcript. — [arXiv 2509.06301](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.06301v2)
- Reliability studies for coding contributing factors in high-hazard industries are rarely done (HFACS ATC study). — [search result](https://ersearch2.cvtisr.sk/vufind/SummonRecord/FETCH-LOGICAL-c362t-a03921f993e3ba4c793f2d45efee0c4c0a36c453933c636e3eeecac61a5a347f3/Details)
- LLM as second coder:
  - GPT-3 with an expert codebook: κ 0.38 (fair) to 0.61 (substantial) against experts, below expert-expert agreement. — [Xiao et al., arXiv 2304.10548](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.10548)
  - GPT-4 Fleiss κ ≈ 0.46 with human coders, below human-human. — [QualiGPT, arXiv 2407.14925](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.14925)

- Cohen's kappa = (p_o − p_e)/(1 − p_e), i.e. agreement corrected for chance. Landis & Koch (1977) bands: ≤0 poor, 0.01–0.20 slight, 0.21–0.40 fair, 0.41–0.60 moderate, 0.61–0.80 substantial, 0.81–1.00 almost perfect. Kappa drops when one category is very common or very rare (prevalence paradox). **[unverified — background knowledge, no source fetched]**

### Inferences
- A single coder's options, ranked by how much they check: (1) a co-coder calibrates the codebook on a pilot subset by negotiated agreement, then one person codes the rest (Campbell); (2) an LLM codes a random sample deductively from the frozen codebook, then compute κ per code and read every disagreement, *which tests the codebook's clarity, not the truth*; (3) re-code a random 10–20% sample after a gap of weeks and compare (intra-rater consistency); (4) an audit trail (codebook versions with dates, memo per new code, decision log of merges and splits). Options 3–4 come from general qualitative practice and are **[unverified]** this session.
- LLM limits for postmortems: likely good on explicit tags ("crunch", "feature creep"), weaker on latent ones ("root cause = people"); it can invent off-codebook labels; agreement with a model shows only that the codebook is clear to a model.

# game-idea-triad

The user's own model of a good game idea (fantasy + logic + aesthetic, aligned), and the advice they turned down.

**Model (user):** a good idea needs a good **fantasy**, **logic** and **aesthetic**, all three **aligned**.
Each one alone is easy to imagine; aligning them is the hard part.
Use their three words.
The three are now joined in a chain (user): logic and aesthetic cause actions, actions cause feelings, and the feelings sell the fantasy.
The chain, its picture and the steps to check an idea against it are in the `fantasy-chain` skill.

**Context:** after dropping Mustache Madness's core in late October 2026, they built and dropped about a game a day (Red Button, Pet the Cat, Deathly Reality Show, ...) and described a creativity block.

**Their history:** 6 months of loops on Mustache Madness, until a problem in the foundation made the fun fall apart.
Since then they stop when a foundation needs too many loops, and try a new foundation about once a day, to spot that kind of problem faster.
They don't know what the problem is; a small change to the foundation moves the whole structure (they compared it to NP-complete problems: fun is easy to check, hard to find).

**Turned down by the user; don't offer these again:**
- Start from one part and derive the other two: they already do it, and changing the aesthetic or the logic redefines the fantasy.
- Anchor on one moment of play: one moment fits one pillar and falls apart when they try to fit two or three; no more ways to align the three in the head.
- Test the parts separately: everything is connected, so don't split the whole.
- Restate the search loop: it's already clear to them.
- Test the core with people in a stripped form (paper, grey boxes, no art) before the build: one friend can't judge it without content, models and movement feel, and their stripped prototypes failed anyway. (user)

**Limits (user):** playtesting is expensive and testers aren't always there, so they fall back on imagination.
A locked-in build feels wrong from the start, and tuning can't repair it.
The aesthetic, movement and game feel must be good enough first, so the game communicates and the user can isolate and judge the logic; that work is how they test the logic, not waste (user).

Kept as written while tidy question Q4 (analogies) is open:
User's restated model (2026-11-09): a hub lowers K, but Red Button (one hub) needed too many loops
and found nothing; with lower K, fun is harder to find; with higher K you move less but the scope
grows. Claude's line-by-line fix (verdict pending): there are two dials. **Size** (scope) is how many
parts and options there are. **K** (coupling) is how much each part depends on the others. One hub
made Red Button small, not loose. In NK research, higher K gives shorter walks to lower peaks, with
the best results at low but nonzero K. Read on the map: Red Button = too small; Mustache Madness =
too big and too coupled. Use the kitchen analogy (pantry = size, how spices affect each other = K).
Correction (user caught it): Claude had marked "one hub lowers K" wrong, against its own earlier
advice. The right version: a hub lowers K only if the spokes *use* the hub without *changing* it
(sides next to the main dish). Spokes that rewrite the hub (sauces poured over it) keep K high. Red
Button was that case: "a narrator keeps changing what the button does".

**Graph model (user; use their words):** scope = number of nodes; coupling = number of connections.
High scope = more content and polish loops; high coupling = more loops to settle the core identity.
A loop = playtest, then change the graph's structure or its parts.
Claude's two additions, approved: connections have a direction (a hub works when it changes the features but they don't change it; Red Button failed that, since a narrator kept changing what the button does); and when scope and coupling are both high, their costs multiply (Mustache Madness, Claude's read).

**Formula (user):** low content can be fun without high coupling between features, because the real fun comes from social interaction with friends, and friends are always different without being built, which is why friendslop works.
Claude added: friends only create that when the hub connects them; keeping players coming back is the limit.

**Claude's ideas with no verdict yet:** check alignment by playing a tiny build with people, not bots; build 3 starting ideas side by side, each with a guess and a point where it gets dropped, then compare them in one session; learn across foundations from a log of the same key choices.

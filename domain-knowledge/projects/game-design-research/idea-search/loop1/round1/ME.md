# Round 1, method E "crossover"

## Method log

**Steps**
1. Read `checks.md` and `round1_parents.md` (8 parents). Nothing else under `research_notes/` or `reports/`.
2. Pulled out each parent's player-on-player act and its plan breaker.
   - Acts: hand the threat's attention to a friend (P1), shove a friend (P2), launch a friend (P3), move cover off one friend onto another (P4), shield a friend with your body (P5), throw something a friend must catch (P6), release and catch on the same beat (P7), a caught player guides a free one (P8).
   - Breakers: the threat picks its target by what it sees (P1), a hazard's kind is revealed and picks which friend fits (P5), the threat goes to noise and who holds the eyes changes (P8), random layout or cracks (P2, P4, P6, P7: mostly physics wobble, so weak).
   - New breakers tried: the threat goes after whoever did the crew's last move, and a private stake.
3. Wrote 30 children with no web search, each fusing one act with a breaker from a different parent (or a new breaker) into one sentence.
4. Ranked them on the gates in order (P, then formula, then R) and kept 8 with 8 different core rules.
5. Ran one web search per kept idea, then searched the ideas that replaced them.

**Raw count:** 30.

**Gates the raw ideas failed most**
- **N, re-skin of a parent (6):** many children slid back into P1's "take the threat off a friend" hot potato: Scarf Snatch, Grudge Bull, Lightning Crouch, Ghost Hug and Curse Touch. Shove Thud kept P2's core.
- **P.2, friend has nothing to answer within seconds (4):** the carried, tackled, caught or calmed player is passive (Storm Ferry, Tackle Save, Floor Catch, Heartbeat Hand).
- **F2, two rules stacked instead of fused (4):** the private-stake breaker never fused; it always added a scoring rule (Ward Oath, Afterlife Caller, Ballroom Spin, Chest Beacon).
- **R.1, a plan card exists (3):** "a colour is revealed, so pass to the matching friend" turns into a fixed reflex (Wire Colour Bomb, Colour Tiles, Colour Shield).

**Novelty searches and swaps**
- Swap 1: Colour Shield (raw 7) has the same core as *Color Cannon Co-op* (itch prototype): a turret fires coloured shots, a colour-matched shield blocks them, and you pass a shot to your partner or reflect it back. Swapped in Held Doors (raw 9).
- Swap 2: Mirror Chain (raw 4) failed on the same game. Its core, bouncing a hazard onto a ready teammate or back at its source, is what *Color Cannon Co-op* does. Swapped in Place Trade (raw 10).
- **Not searched:** Held Doors, Place Trade and Nobody Hides Alone. The session's web-search budget ran out after 7 searches, so N is unchecked for these three.
- **Near matches, kept as ~:**
  - Swap Coats vs *Tokyo Wanted*: there you trade looks with crowd NPCs and the poster updates on a report. Here only a friend can change your look.
  - Giant's Wand vs *Shiftlings*: two players on an air hose swap who is big. Here you point at any friend, and being made big is what makes the giant grab you.
  - Hedge Toss vs *HupHup* (throw a friend up a mountain) and *Fly You Fools* (one player maps, one explores): neither has a throw that gives the thrown player the map.
- **No match found:** One Lamp, Red Dot.

**Good / bad**
- **Good:** the best children came from fusing an act with a "who does the threat go for" breaker into one object that both helps and hurts the friend who gets it: the lamp is sight plus bait, size is power plus bait, a coat is cover plus a wanted look.
- **Bad:**
  - The parents' acts are narrow (mostly bodies moving), so the children cluster.
  - Four of the 8 kept ideas use the same breaker type: the threat goes for whoever did the last thing (lamp, door, shut, send). Hedge Toss and Red Dot use a lighter form of it.
  - A private reason never fused into a one-sentence rule.
  - Crossover pulls hard toward hot potato, so a hard rule against "pass the danger" would help.

## Kept ideas

### ME-1 One Lamp
- Rule (one sentence): Only the player holding the lamp can see, and the thing in the dark always stalks the lamp, so handing it to a friend gives them both the sight and the hunter.
- Fantasy (who you are, what you're doing): a gang of young burglars clearing a dead lord's manor of its silver in one night, with one oil lamp between them, while something that lives in the dark moves through the house.
- Aesthetic (one line): a black screen except one warm circle of lamplight, with dusty portraits, long shadows and two eyes glinting at the edge.
- Player on player (what my action does to a friend, and what they decide back):
  - I hand the lamp to Mia, or slide it across the floor to her. She can now see, I am blind, and the thing turns toward her.
  - Within seconds she must choose: run with it, or pass it on to someone farther from the thing.
  - Passing it to a friend who is cornered hurts them. Passing it to a friend next to an unexplored room helps them.
  - The holder has one hand, so cannot carry silver. The blind players carry it, guided only by the holder's words.
- Why the plan can't settle (what changes round to round because of the players):
  - The manor is generated and dark, so a room exists for the crew only once the lamp has been there.
  - The thing follows the lamp, so it is wherever the crew has just been.
  - Who should hold the lamp next depends on who is farthest from the thing and nearest the next silver, and that changes every few seconds.
  - The blind players need the holder's description of what is in front of them every time they move.
- Hub (the one shared thing everyone uses): the lamp (one rule: sight for the holder, a beacon for the thing). Extra rule: the holder can't carry silver.
- At 3 / at 6:
  - At 3: one lamp and two blind carriers, so every pass leaves the new holder two people to talk through the room.
  - At 6: two lamps. The thing goes for the nearer lamp, so the two holders steer it between them.
- Hidden (what, how uncovered, why hide): the rooms and the thing are hidden in the dark and uncovered only by lamplight. The crew hides by passing the light away from where the thing is heading.
- A failed run (what happens, who is blamed): Tom throws the lamp to Ana as the thing reaches him. It lands short, and Ana is left in the dark one step from it. Everyone saw the lamp's arc from Tom's hand.
- Online lag: a thrown lamp needs one authority on where it lands.

### ME-2 Giant's Wand
- Rule (one sentence): You can grow or shrink any friend you point at but never yourself, and the giant always grabs the biggest player it can see.
- Fantasy (who you are, what you're doing): tiny thieves stealing the golden egg from a giant's kitchen. Each has a wand that works only on other people.
- Aesthetic (one line): a huge candlelit kitchen with crumbs like boulders, a dripping tap like a waterfall, and the giant's slippers thundering past.
- Player on player (what my action does to a friend, and what they decide back):
  - I make Leo huge so he can shove the cheese wheel off the trapdoor. He is now the biggest thing the giant can see.
  - He has seconds to finish and shout for someone to shrink him, or to duck behind the dresser.
  - I can shrink Ana while she hangs from a shelf, and she drops.
  - I can make Mia big in front of a smaller friend, so Mia's body blocks the giant's view of them.
- Why the plan can't settle (what changes round to round because of the players):
  - The kitchen is generated: mouse holes need someone tiny, heavy lids need someone huge, high shelves need a big friend to stand on.
  - The giant wanders and turns toward whoever is biggest in view.
  - So which friend should be big right now depends on where the giant is looking and who stands by which obstacle. Being made big is both the tool and the bait.
- Hub (the one shared thing everyone uses): size (tiny, normal, huge) and the giant (one rule: grab the biggest player in sight). Extra rule: a size wears off after 15 s.
- At 3 / at 6:
  - At 3: each player has two friends to aim at, and one big friend at a time is about all the crew can risk.
  - At 6: several jobs need a big player at once, so the crew must choose who is the biggest bait.
- Hidden (what, how uncovered, why hide): none (the giant's view is shown).
- A failed run (what happens, who is blamed): Ana makes Tom huge just as the giant turns around. He is snatched and dropped in the soup pot. Everyone saw Ana's wand flash at him.

### ME-3 Hedge Toss
- Rule (one sentence): Only a player thrown up above the hedge maze can see its layout, so you hurl a friend skyward and they shout the way before they land.
- Fantasy (who you are, what you're doing): festival-goers lost at night in a living hedge maze, hunted by the gardener's beast, trying to reach the exit gate.
- Aesthetic (one line): a moonlit topiary maze seen from behind the shoulder, then a dizzying top-down glimpse at the top of each throw.
- Player on player (what my action does to a friend, and what they decide back):
  - I pick Ana up and throw her, choosing the angle and the power.
  - At the top of her flight she gets about 2 seconds of the map. She leans to steer her fall and calls the route and where the beast is.
  - A throw that lands her on the right path ahead helps her. A throw over the wrong hedge drops her by the beast.
  - Once she lands, she must throw someone else. The thrower is always left behind and needs the next throw.
- Why the plan can't settle (what changes round to round because of the players):
  - The hedges shift every minute, so a route seen goes stale fast.
  - The beast walks to each landing thud, so throwing from the same spot brings it to the crew.
  - Each throw shows a different part of the maze to a different player, who must tell the others.
- Hub (the one shared thing everyone uses): the maze (one generator plus a shift timer) and the throw (carry a friend, release by angle and power). Extra rule: the beast goes to landing thuds.
- At 3 / at 6:
  - At 3: one thrower, one flyer and one waiting to be thrown, so nobody is spare.
  - At 6: two or three throwing pairs in different spots compare what they saw.
- Hidden (what, how uncovered, why hide): the layout and the beast are hidden behind hedges and uncovered only by being thrown up. The crew wants to hide because the beast hunts by sound, and every landing gives a position away.
- A failed run (what happens, who is blamed): Leo throws Mia over the wrong hedge, straight into the beast's corridor. Everyone saw Leo's throw arc.
- Online lag: the thrown arc needs one authority.

### ME-4 Swap Coats
- Rule (one sentence): You can't change your own clothes, only trade one piece with a friend you touch, and each guard hunts the look it last saw commit a crime.
- Fantasy (who you are, what you're doing): a gang of pickpockets working a crowded masked carnival.
- Aesthetic (one line): a lantern-lit canal carnival, coloured cloaks, hats and masks, and a paper wanted poster fluttering over each guard's head.
- Player on player (what my action does to a friend, and what they decide back):
  - Tom just lifted a purse in front of a guard, who now hunts "red hat, blue cloak, cat mask".
  - I brush past Tom and we trade hats. He no longer matches, but now I'm wearing the red hat.
  - Within seconds Tom must decide whom to pass the blue cloak to.
  - A trade can help a friend (break their match) or hurt them (hand them the piece that completes another guard's poster).
- Why the plan can't settle (what changes round to round because of the players):
  - Each guard carries its own poster, made from the last crime it saw, so the posters are the crew's own past moves.
  - Every steal rewrites a poster, which changes which trades are safe and who should steal next.
  - Nobody can fix their own look, so every rescue goes through a friend.
- Hub (the one shared thing everyone uses): clothing (3 slots, a few colours each) and the guards (one rule: arrest anyone nearby who matches 2 of the 3 pieces on its poster). Extra rule: the crowd is simple wandering NPCs.
- At 3 / at 6: at 3 there are few pieces, so every trade changes a lot. At 6 there are more pieces and more guards, and posters overlap.
- Hidden (what, how uncovered, why hide): a guard's poster shows only when you are close to that guard, so you uncover it by getting near. You hide your crimes from guards' sight.
- A failed run (what happens, who is blamed): Ana trades her cat mask to Leo right beside the guard whose poster says "cat mask, green cloak", and he is grabbed. Everyone saw the trade.

### ME-5 Held Doors
- Rule (one sentence): Doors stay open only while someone holds them, and the hound always runs at whoever is holding one.
- Fantasy (who you are, what you're doing): kitchen servants who stole from the duke's table, fleeing his mad hound through a castle of heavy spring doors.
- Aesthetic (one line): torchlit stone corridors, heavy oak doors slamming, and a huge shaggy hound skidding on flagstones.
- Player on player (what my action does to a friend, and what they decide back):
  - I hold the cellar door for Ana, and the hound turns toward me.
  - Ana must run through and take the door from the far side, so I can let go and slip through before the hound arrives.
  - If I let go early, she is shut in on the hound's side. If she is slow, I'm caught.
  - Holding a side door anywhere pulls the hound off a cornered friend.
- Why the plan can't settle (what changes round to round because of the players):
  - The castle is generated and branches, and each closed door hides the next room.
  - The hound goes to whoever holds a door, so every hold both lets someone through and steers the hound.
  - Who should hold which door depends on where the hound and each friend are at that second.
- Hub (the one shared thing everyone uses): the doors (one rule: open only while held) and the hound (one rule: run at the nearest holder).
- At 3 / at 6:
  - At 3: every door needs a hand-off, and the third player is the lure.
  - At 6: many doors are held at once and the hound picks the nearest holder, so holds must be timed so they don't pull it onto the wrong friend.
- Hidden (what, how uncovered, why hide): the room behind each closed door, uncovered only by opening it. Opening is risky because holding the door calls the hound.
- A failed run (what happens, who is blamed): Tom lets go of the cellar door while Ana is still in the frame. It slams, and she is stuck on the hound's side. Everyone saw Tom's hand come off the door.
- Novelty: not searched (web-search budget ran out).

### ME-6 Red Dot

- Fantasy (who you are, what you're doing): a gang of outlaws dragging a stolen strongbox down the main street of a hostile frontier town while a sharpshooter works from the rooftops.
- Aesthetic (one line): a dusty western street at noon, sun glints from the bell tower, and cast-iron frying pans held up as shields.
- Player on player (what my action does to a friend, and what they decide back):
  - The glint lands on Ana. She runs to put Tom between herself and the tower, and Tom steps into the line with his pan. Both decide within seconds.
  - A marked player can also duck behind a friend without asking, which puts that friend in the line. If their pan is already broken, the friend takes the bullet.
- Why the plan can't settle (what changes round to round because of the players):
  - The sharpshooter marks whoever stands least covered, so it answers the crew's formation.
  - It also moves to a new rooftop, shown in the round, so the safe side of every body flips.
  - Pans break, so who can still block changes as the run goes on.
- Hub (the one shared thing everyone uses): the line from the shooter to the marked player. Extra rule: a pan breaks after one hit, and a new one can be grabbed from a store porch.
- At 3 / at 6:
  - At 3: one shooter, and every mark leaves exactly two possible blockers.
  - At 6: two shooters on opposite roofs, so blocking for one exposes a friend to the other.
- Hidden (what, how uncovered, why hide): which roof the shooter is on, uncovered only by the glint when it marks someone. The shooter hides itself, and the crew hides behind walls and each other.
- A failed run (what happens, who is blamed): Leo ducks behind Mia, whose pan is already broken, and she takes the shot. Everyone saw Leo duck.

### ME-7 Nobody Hides Alone
- Rule (one sentence): A hiding place shuts only from outside, and the witch goes to the last door she heard shut.
- Fantasy (who you are, what you're doing): village children sneaking through a witch's crooked cottage to free their pets from her cages.
- Aesthetic (one line): a lopsided candlelit cottage full of wardrobes, chests and ovens, and keyhole views of the witch's long shadow.
- Player on player (what my action does to a friend, and what they decide back):
  - As the witch comes near, I push Ana into the wardrobe and shut it. She is hidden, but the slam calls the witch to her wardrobe.
  - Through the keyhole Ana sees the witch coming. She must shout for someone to shut another door elsewhere, or burst out and run.
  - Whoever shuts the last door has nobody left to shut them in.
- Why the plan can't settle (what changes round to round because of the players):
  - The witch reacts only to the crew's own door shuts, so every hide sets her next target.
  - The cottage is generated.
  - Who ends up as the last one outside is settled live, by who is nearest the witch and which doors are still free.
- Hub (the one shared thing everyone uses): the hiding places (one rule: shut only from outside) and the witch (one rule: walk to the last shut sound and grab whoever she sees).
- At 3 / at 6:
  - At 3: there is always one player outside, and each shut moves the danger.
  - At 6: many shuts make the witch bounce around, so the crew must agree whose door slams next.
- Hidden (what, how uncovered, why hide): which hiding place holds a child, uncovered only when the witch opens it. The children hide because she grabs anyone she sees, and hidden players see the room through the keyhole and call her moves.
- A failed run (what happens, who is blamed): Tom slams the trunk on Mia while the witch is in the next room. She walks straight in and opens it. Everyone heard Tom's slam.
- Novelty: not searched (web-search budget ran out). Idle risk: a hidden player is only watching until the witch comes.

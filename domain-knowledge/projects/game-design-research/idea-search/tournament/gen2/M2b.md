# M2b: real-world joint activities (gen 2 rerun)

## Method log
- **Steps.**
  1. Listed 40 real activities where a small group does different, timed actions and one slip shows
     (trades, old industries, farm work, rescue, stage work, rituals, sports drills). Skipped cooking.
  2. Wrote each one's coupling: who does what, what each needs from the others, how failure shows.
  3. Turned each into a one-sentence rule; fantasy and look come from the activity.
  4. Scored each roughly against the checks, kept 8 with 8 different core rules.
  5. Novelty step: one web search per kept idea, swaps below, then one search per replacement.
- **Raw count:** 40.
- **Checks the raw candidates failed most:**
  - F3 (17 of 40 tagged ✗ or pooled, 5 more ~). Real group work is mostly pooled force (hauling, lifting, carrying a net or a
    sheet), a follow-the-leader chain (dragon dance), or one side that obeys calls (crane, hoist,
    stretcher lowering, blacksmith's striker).
  - N (9 of 40). Stage, sport and transport activities are often already games (Octodad, Embr,
    Derail Valley, Moving Out, Ringing Room).
  - F7 (4 of 40 tagged, and all 8 kept ideas needed a tail added). A real task repeats the same cycle (oil-rig pipe tripping, Double Dutch, maypole),
    so the tail has to come from generated ground or a simple system, not from the activity itself.
- **Swaps after the novelty search (11 searches):**
  - Flying trapeze relay → out. *Men on the Flying Trapeze* (Kloonigames, 2010) is a co-op trapeze
    game built on release-and-catch timing between partners: the same core rule. Replaced by
    **On Air** (M2b-6); search found no co-op game with a locked live camera handed between players.
  - Arborist rigging → out. A Steam game (app 5446560) is a 1–4 player online co-op physics game
    about taking trees down by roping limbs down piece by piece without wrecking what's below: the
    same core rule. Replaced by **Looping Mill** (M2b-7); search found no rolling-mill co-op game.
  - Beach seine haul → out. The playground game *Fishermen and Fish* (catchers hold hands as a net
    and close it around the runners) has the same core rule. Super Mario Party's *Net Worth* is
    close too. Replaced by **Club Passing** (M2b-8); search found only jam games tagged "juggling"
    with no passing rule described, and *Don't Drop It* (throw-and-catch of one object, 2 players).
  - Kept after search, with notes. Megalith rollers, rivet gang, stage fight: nothing close found.
    Hump yard: *Hump Yard* is a competitive board game about sorting freight cars into trains. No
    riding, braking or live switch timing, so a different core rule. Misdirection: no co-op magic
    show found, but co-op stealth games use "one distracts, one sneaks" as a side tool, so N may
    score ~ there.
- **Where I expect points lost (self-check, unscored):** M2b-8 F7 ~ (the tail is mostly the
  players plus one moving blocker); M2b-7 F3 ~ and F4 ~ (everyone does the same catch-and-feed job,
  and a dropped head can be blamed on two people); M2b-5 N ~; M2b-1 F3 has a pooled part (the rope
  pull), but the core coupling, laying the logs, is not pooled.
- **Good at:** fantasy and look come free and fit the logic. Old industries and trades gave two-way
  couplings with real timing windows (switch gap vs. braking, hot rivet vs. bucker, log relay vs.
  pull speed). Old industries were the most novel source. 6 of 8 different families (transport,
  construction, ancient labour, stage, broadcast, circus).
- **Bad at:** the most common real coupling is pooled force, so many raw ideas failed F3. Real
  activities repeat one cycle, so every kept idea needed a generated layout or a simple system added
  for F7. Real crew sizes (2 or 4) rarely fit 3–6. Stage, sport and rescue drift toward existing
  games. It also missed semi-co-op: no real activity on the list came with a private stake or a
  traitor.

## Kept ideas

### M2b-1 Megalith Haul
- Rule (one sentence): A huge stone slides only over logs, and each log that rolls out behind it must be carried round and laid in front before the stone reaches bare ground.
- Hub (the one shared thing everyone uses): the stone on its row of rollers.
- Coupling (how my best move depends on what a friend just did; who does which different action): Rope players pull the stone and set its pace. Runners grab the log freed at the back, run it forward and lay it, and the angle they lay it at turns the stone. As a runner I lay my log to fix or continue the turn the last runner made. As a rope player I slow down when no runner is coming with a log. On a downhill the stone rolls by itself, and rope players must switch to the back ropes and hold it, so the jobs flip with the ground. 2 extra rules: rope pull, log angle steers.
- Fantasy (who you are, what you're doing): a stone-age village moving a standing stone to the henge before the solstice sunrise.
- Look (one line): pale chalk hills at dawn, a grey slab riding on pale logs, hide ropes, people in wool.
- At 3 / at 6: 3 = 1 on the rope, 2 runners, slow stone, short path. 6 = 3 rope, 3 runners, faster stone, longer path. Anyone can drop a rope and become a runner.
- Hidden (what, how it's uncovered, why anyone hides it) or "none": none.
- A failed run (what happens, who is blamed): the stone's front edge passes the last log and digs in, or tips off a skewed log into a ditch. The gap is where one runner was due, still holding their log, or the rope player who kept pulling; everyone sees it.
- Tail (what makes round 50 different from round 2, with no hand-made content): generated ground each haul (slopes, mud where logs sink, boulders and ditches to steer round). Logs split after a few passes, so supply varies. A skewed log turns the stone, so no two hauls take the same line.

### M2b-2 Rivet Gang
- Rule (one sentence): Every joint on the steel frame needs a rivet thrown up red-hot from the forge, caught, and hammered while a second player holds the other side of the same hole, all before it cools.
- Hub (the one shared thing everyone uses): the frame's open rivet holes (each hot rivet carries a cooling clock).
- Coupling (how my best move depends on what a friend just did; who does which different action): The heater times each rivet in the fire (too short, not hot; too long, burnt) and throws it to whichever pair is ready. The catcher must be where the arc lands and set the rivet in a hole of their choice. The second player must reach the far side of that same hole before it cools. The heater picks a target by who is free and in reach. The catcher moves by the throw. The far-side player moves by the catcher's chosen hole. 2 extra rules: the crane swings in the next beam on its own clock, and wind sways the beams and the throws.
- Fantasy (who you are, what you're doing): 1930s ironworkers raising a skyscraper on bare girders.
- Look (one line): rust-red steel over a hazy skyline, glowing rivets arcing through dusk.
- At 3 / at 6: 3 = heater plus one pair on the beam; every rivet needs all three. 6 = heater plus two or three pairs on different beams; the heater chooses who gets each hot rivet, and pairs reform on the fly.
- Hidden (what, how it's uncovered, why anyone hides it) or "none": none.
- A failed run (what happens, who is blamed): a beam still on its temporary bolts tears loose when the crane lets go, because too few rivets went in. Each lost rivet was seen falling glowing to the street: a bad throw (heater), a missed catch (catcher), or cold because the far side was empty (that player).
- Tail (what makes round 50 different from round 2, with no hand-made content): a generated frame each floor (beam layout, hole positions, which girders connect for walking). Wind gusts. The building grows, so throws get longer until someone carries the forge up a floor, which leaves a gap with no heater.

### M2b-3 Hump Yard
- Rule (one sentence): Freight cars roll one by one off a hill into a fan of sidings, and players throw the switches to send each car to its colour's track and ride the cars to brake them by hand so they couple gently.
- Hub (the one shared thing everyone uses): the switch ladder below the hill.
- Coupling (how my best move depends on what a friend just did; who does which different action): A switch can be thrown only in the gap between two cars, and the gap depends on how hard the rider ahead is braking. A rider who brakes early to avoid a hard coupling shrinks the gap for the switch tender behind. A late tender forces the rider of the next car to brake hard to buy time. Riders run back up the hill after each car, so who rides which car is called out on the fly. 2 extra rules: heavy cars roll faster, and a full siding forces a reroute.
- Fantasy (who you are, what you're doing): the night shift at a 1950s freight yard, brakemen and switchmen with lanterns.
- Look (one line): sodium lights, steam, wet rails, boxcars painted by destination.
- At 3 / at 6: 3 = 1 tender running between 3 switch stands, 2 riders, 4 sidings. 6 = 2–3 tenders, 3–4 riders, 7 sidings. Your job is wherever you stand.
- Hidden (what, how it's uncovered, why anyone hides it) or "none": none (car weight is chalked on the side).
- A failed run (what happens, who is blamed): a switch thrown under a car derails it (the tender, seen at the lever); a car slams into the cut at speed and its cargo bursts (its rider, seen jumping off); a car stops short and fouls the ladder (its rider braked too much). Three wrecks end the shift.
- Tail (what makes round 50 different from round 2, with no hand-made content): a generated train each shift (car order, destinations, weights). Sidings fill as the night goes on, so roll distances shrink and change. The hill engine pushes faster over the shift.

### M2b-4 Stage Fight
- Rule (one sentence): In a staged brawl every attack is wound up in plain view toward one body zone, and its target must show the matching block before it lands; a blow that lands is a real injury.
- Hub (the one shared thing everyone uses): the stage, watched by one simple audience that leaves when the fight goes slow or repeats.
- Coupling (how my best move depends on what a friend just did; who does which different action): The attacker picks a target, a zone and a wind-up speed. A fast wind-up thrills the audience but is harder to read, and repeating a move bores them. The defender reads the wind-up and blocks, or sells it with a dramatic fall that thrills but leaves them down for 2 seconds. My best attack depends on who is free (not already blocking someone), who is down, and which moves were just used. Two attacks on one defender at once is the main danger, so attackers must watch each other. 1 extra rule: the audience.
- Fantasy (who you are, what you're doing): stunt actors on opening night of a swashbuckling play, faking a tavern brawl.
- Look (one line): footlights, a painted tavern set, wooden swords, a dark crowd that cheers or starts to leave.
- At 3 / at 6: 3 = a triangle; everyone attacks and defends, and two attacks on one person are easy to spot. 6 = crossing pairs and chains, more ways to double up on one defender, and more moves to keep fresh.
- Hidden (what, how it's uncovered, why anyone hides it) or "none": none (the wind-up shows the zone).
- A failed run (what happens, who is blamed): a blow lands for real; the actor yelps and the prop sword cracks. It's seen whether the wind-up was unreadable (attacker) or the wrong zone was blocked (defender). Three injuries or an empty house ends the show.
- Tail (what makes round 50 different from round 2, with no hand-made content): the audience punishes repeats, so the crew must keep inventing new sequences; the players are the tail. Set props come from a short list (stairs, tables, a chandelier rope), placed at random, and change which zones can be reached.

### M2b-5 Misdirection
- Rule (one sentence): The audience always looks at the biggest motion on stage, and a secret move only works if nobody in the audience is looking at it.
- Hub (the one shared thing everyone uses): the audience's gaze, one simple system shown as heads turning.
- Coupling (how my best move depends on what a friend just did; who does which different action): Flourishers pull eyes with showy moves; repeating the same flourish pulls less. Sneaks do the hidden part of the trick (slip through the trapdoor, swap the box, palm the dove). A sneak moves the moment a flourish has pulled the nearby heads away. A flourisher must draw eyes away from wherever the sneak goes next, and a flourish too close to the sneak drags eyes onto them. 1 extra rule: flourish fatigue.
- Fantasy (who you are, what you're doing): a travelling magic troupe on a Victorian music-hall stage.
- Look (one line): velvet curtains, limelight, a sea of top hats whose heads visibly swivel.
- At 3 / at 6: 3 = one trick at a time, roles swap between tricks. 6 = two tricks at once on opposite sides of the stage, so one trick's flourish can expose the other trick's sneak.
- Hidden (what, how it's uncovered, why anyone hides it) or "none": none between players (secret moves are hidden only from the audience, and players see the gaze).
- A failed run (what happens, who is blamed): a head snaps to the sneak mid-move, gasps and points; the trick is blown. Seen in the moment: the sneak moved while eyes were near, or a flourisher stopped too early or flourished too close. Three blown tricks and the troupe is booed off.
- Tail (what makes round 50 different from round 2, with no hand-made content): the gaze tires of repeated flourishes, so the crew must vary them. Each night's act is drawn from a short list of about 15 tricks (which prop or person must end where) on 2–3 stage layouts.

### M2b-6 On Air
- Rule (one sentence): Every player runs a camera at a chaotic live event, and the camera on air is locked still, so its operator must hand the broadcast to another camera that already has the action framed before the action leaves the shot.
- Hub (the one shared thing everyone uses): the broadcast feed, shown to all.
- Coupling (how my best move depends on what a friend just did; who does which different action): While I'm live I can't move, and I choose when and to whom I hand off. Everyone else must guess where the action goes next and be framed there. A handoff to someone not framed puts their empty shot on air. Where I should stand depends on who is live and where their frame will lose the action. The live seat moves around the crew with every handoff, so nobody directs for long. 1 extra rule: crowds and obstacles block sight lines.
- Fantasy (who you are, what you're doing): a small local TV crew covering a runaway-bull fiesta, live.
- Look (one line): a grainy 1980s TV feed with the station logo and the live camera's number in the corner.
- At 3 / at 6: 3 = every handoff needs one of only two others, so both must keep moving. 6 = more coverage, but more choice of who to hand to, and two cameras going for one spot block each other.
- Hidden (what, how it's uncovered, why anyone hides it) or "none": none (all camera views are shown as small tiles).
- A failed run (what happens, who is blamed): dead air, with an empty plaza on the broadcast and a number in the corner. Either the live operator held too long, or they handed to a camera that wasn't framed, and that camera's number is on the empty shot. Three dead-air strikes and the station pulls the feed.
- Tail (what makes round 50 different from round 2, with no hand-made content): the bull is one simple system (charges, swerves, stops at random), so its path never repeats. Venues are 2–3 layouts with crowds and carts placed at random.

### M2b-7 Looping Mill
- Rule (one sentence): Glowing steel rods snake through a mill of roller stands, and whenever a rod's head pops out of a stand a player must catch it with tongs and feed it into another stand before it whips loose, while the hot loop left between the two stands sweeps across the floor.
- Hub (the one shared thing everyone uses): the mill floor and the rods on it.
- Coupling (how my best move depends on what a friend just did; who does which different action): Each rod needs a set number of passes through any stands. Feeding into a far stand makes a long loop that cuts across the floor. My path to the next head runs across loops my friends just made. The stand I pick decides whose path my loop blocks and where the rod's head pops out next. Several rods run at once with heads due at different moments, so who takes which head is called out live. 1 extra rule: touching a loop burns you and you drop your tongs.
- Fantasy (who you are, what you're doing): catchers in an 1890s rod mill.
- Look (one line): a dark iron hall, orange rods looping across black floor plates, sparks.
- At 3 / at 6: 3 = 4 stands, 1–2 rods at a time. 6 = 7 stands, 3–4 rods, and the floor fills with loops.
- Hidden (what, how it's uncovered, why anyone hides it) or "none": none.
- A failed run (what happens, who is blamed): a head isn't caught in time and the rod cobbles, whipping across the floor. It's seen who called that head and didn't get there, or whose long loop cut off their path. Three cobbles and the mill shuts down.
- Tail (what makes round 50 different from round 2, with no hand-made content): loop physics (rope sim). Stands are placed at random on a grid each shift. The rod schedule speeds up. The floor pattern is never the same twice.

### M2b-8 Club Passing
- Rule (one sentence): On every beat each player holding a club throws one to a teammate, and it lands only if the catcher is facing the thrower that beat and has a free hand.
- Hub (the one shared thing everyone uses): the beat, and the clubs in the air.
- Coupling (how my best move depends on what a friend just did; who does which different action): As thrower I pick someone who will have a free hand and face me. As catcher I pick whom to face, which picks the one club I can take; two throws at one catcher means one drops. A clown rolls across the ring on a random path and blocks the lines between players, so a fixed pattern breaks and the crew must reroute live. More clubs come in over time. 2 extra rules: the rolling clown, rising club count.
- Fantasy (who you are, what you're doing): circus jugglers passing clubs, then torches, in the big top.
- Look (one line): sawdust ring, spotlight, striped clubs spinning between players.
- At 3 / at 6: 3 = a triangle with 4–5 clubs, few routes, so one block forces an instant reroute. 6 = a ring of 6 with 9–10 clubs, many routes, and collisions are likely.
- Hidden (what, how it's uncovered, why anyone hides it) or "none": none.
- A failed run (what happens, who is blamed): a club hits the sawdust. It's seen who threw it and whether the catcher was facing someone else or had full hands. Three drops end the show.
- Tail (what makes round 50 different from round 2, with no hand-made content): the clown's random path and the rising club count keep breaking whatever pattern the crew settles on. Players can walk the ring, so the lines between them change. The routing is the players' own; there is no hand-made content.
